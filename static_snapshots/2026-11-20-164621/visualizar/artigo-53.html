Título extraído: A foie gras farm in New York state just burned down

Early Thursday morning in upstate New York, a fire broke out at Hudson Valley Foie Gras — the largest US producer of foie gras. French for “fatty liver,” foie gras is a delicacy product that’s made by force-feeding ducks in the final weeks of their lives.

The fire destroyed multiple barns, and while hundreds of young ducks and geese survived, it’s still unknown how many died. Commercial duck farms tend to house thousands of animals — and sometimes over 10,000 animals — per barn.

“The fire took off so fast, there was no way to get inside to stop the spread,” Charlie Kalinowski, chief of the Smallwood/Mongaup Valley Fire Department, told Radio Catskill.

Approximately 200 first responders from 37 fire departments responded to the blaze, and no humans were injured. Hudson Valley Foie Gras didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment, though a vice president at the company told Radio Catskill that they suspect it started due to an electrical malfunction, which hasn’t been confirmed by local fire officials.

Allie Granger of the nonprofit Animal Welfare Institute told Vox that the cause of barn fires usually goes unreported, but when they are reported, electrical malfunction tends to be among the most common causes at relatively large operations like Hudson Valley Foie Gras.

This incident is expected to hit the US foie gras supply hard, as Hudson Valley Foie Gras is one of just two major US producers. The fire came weeks after voters in Washington, DC, and Denver, Colorado, voted to ban the sale of foie gras. Foie gras sales have also been banned in California; Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania; Portland, Oregon; New York City; and the country of Brazil. India has banned imports of the product.

The fire may get a relatively large amount of press attention, given the decades-long controversy over foie gras and recent votes on the product. But barn fires are a surprisingly common occurrence in the US livestock sector, killing hundreds of thousands to over a million animals each year. Some steps could be taken to reduce that number, though the meat, milk, and egg industries have largely opposed reforms.

America’s industrial barn fire problem, briefly explained

In 2026, according to the Animal Welfare Institute, more than 1.2 million animals have already died in over 80 farm fires in the US. About 1 million of them died when several barns burned down at a massive egg farm in Iowa in March.

All told, more than 10 million farmed animals have died in fires in the US since 2013, which AWI believes is an undercount since reporting requirements vary by state.

When fires break out at farms, there’s usually no escape route for the animals. As I wrote in 2024:

Virtually all animals raised for meat, dairy, and eggs in the US are raised on factory farms, where thousands to tens of thousands of animals are crammed together in large warehouses. These aren’t the old red barns you might see from the highway, anachronisms from a pre-industrial age. These contain modern ventilation, lighting, and heating systems that can malfunction and start a fire.

According to the National Fire Protection Association, malfunctioning heating and electrical systems are the leading cause of US barn fires. The organization writes fire codes and standards, which are then adopted by states and localities, and they have a code for animal housing facilities, which covers farms. Granger told me it includes guidelines for sprinkler systems, building construction and materials, smoke detection, fire alarm systems and notification, among other measures.

In 2024, the National Fire Protection Association voted to update its animal housing code to require sprinklers in large barns. Industry trade groups representing the egg, pork, and chicken meat industries opposed the decision. The vote was arguably symbolic, though; farmers would only need to follow it in states that have adopted the code. According to NFPA, only nine states have — New York isn’t one of them — and several of these actually exempt farms from it. (Among the states that don’t exempt farms, it’s unclear whether the sprinkler requirement is being enforced. The NFPA didn’t immediately respond to questions.)

Industry groups have argued that sprinkler systems would be too costly, that some farms may not have the water infrastructure to supply them in the event of a fire, and that other fire prevention and mitigation measures are sufficient. It’s currently unknown whether or not Hudson Valley Foie Gras’s barns had sprinkler systems.

“We require sprinklers in so many different facilities across the United States,” Granger said. “They have been proven to be lifesaving.”

The ducks and geese that died in the fire at Hudson Valley Foie Gras this week were of course destined to be slaughtered. But their deaths are a reminder of how little we know about the lives and safety of the animals US farms raise for our meat, milk, and egg supply.
