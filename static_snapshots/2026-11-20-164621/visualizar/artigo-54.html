Título extraído: Gen Z is getting nostalgic for the Obama era - with an asterisk

Millennial optimism is making a comeback online, thanks to the Democrats’ blue tsunami in this month’s midterm election. Social media is filled with videos, songs, and edits celebrating the midterms and linking the feeling to Barack Obama’s victories and the various cultural signifiers of the time, from indie bands to “girlboss” CEOs. Many of them are produced by Gen Z users who have little memory of the era themselves.

To some left-leaning millennials, it’s a teachable moment. On Instagram the day after the election, 37-year-old Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) gave her younger followers a lesson in the roots of “millennial cringe,” that mix of hope and earnestness that Gen Zers often see as cloying and naive.

“Imagine all the seats we flipped last night, that feeling, plus on top of it, electing the first Black president ever. And on top of that, you did it the first time you ever voted? That hits generationally,” Ocasio-Cortez said. “Like, you are chasing that feeling ever since. And because you did it the first time you ever voted, you’re like, ‘Yeah, we can change the world. Yeah, why not. We did it the first time. Why not just do it again?’”

But while some millennials (including many of the victorious candidates) might proudly claim this moment as a restoration of their culture — everyone back to Hamilton! — some of the Gen Z voters and operatives who helped power their wins are also trying to carve out their own lane.

Rather than a straightforward revival, these Gen Z are calling their experience “WhimZ,” which they see as distinct from millennial optimism, and hardened by their own divergent experiences in the Trump era.

Unlike millennial optimism — rooted in the fact that if you believe in change and progress and work to make it possible, it will come — Gen Z liberals know that’s not the case. And indeed, there’s a sharp satirical edge to some of the stomp-clap celebrations making the rounds.

Obama’s young voters struggled to keep up the same energy, urgency, and turnout (the 2014 midterms had historically low participation) when he wasn’t on the ballot, in part — in “WhimZ’s” telling — because they were overly confident that emerging trends in technology, social media, and culture might solve problems and eliminate discrimination for them.

“Gen Z’s ‘WhimZ’ is very different than millennial optimism and the reason why is us as Gen Z know that we still have to fight,” TikToker @bearclawharley explained in one video. “This is no hate to millennials; you guys were promised…follow the rules and you guys will get a good job and yada yada. We as Gen Z know that that’s not going to happen. You guys were just carefree; we are ironically doing this.”

First-time voters in 2026 were born the year Obama was elected and just 8 years old when Trump was elected in 2016. They’ve grown up in a divisive political climate and understand that even when you march, protest, organize, and ultimately vote — you don’t always get the outcome you want.

Lauren Kapp, who co-founded the Democratic digital strategy firm Luminary Strategies and helped with the James Talarico campaign’s social media operation this midterms, said one of her own core political memories was walking into her high school class after the 2016 election and seeing her AP government teacher in tears.

“Political instability has left Gen Z burned,” Kapp said. “We’ve had to cope through our humor as a matter of processing. To me, WhimZ is less about believing things will get better and more about finding moments of joy despite the uncertainty.”

The rising generation is also not as liberal or partisan — in fact, more than half (56 percent) of Zoomers identify as independent, according to Gallup — and those who do tilt left are more comfortable with anti-establishment voices, like the rising socialist movement, than their elders are. They care far less about party labels and far more about results.

According to Olivia Julianna, a Democratic strategist and content creator, millennial optimism is “more institutional than WhimZ.”

“Millennial optimism exists because Obama won elections so decisively that they had time to hit the club afterward. Then they could go to urgent care for IV fluids on their parents’ health insurance the next morning,” she said, alluding to Gen Z’s lack of trust across institutions. “WhimZ comes from a place that’s a little more vengeful. Our greatest victories have largely come from stopping the worst shit from happening instead of witnessing massive moments of inspiration from people in power or elected officials.”

Rather than remembering elections as their touchstones, Gen Z’s core political memories have been direct actions they took themselves: March for Our Lives, Black Lives Matter, climate protests, or more recently, organized action against ICE.

This sentiment comes up repeatedly in conversations and focus groups with younger voters as part of my research work: Voting the right politicians into office is not enough.

“We know that we have a lot to work on and flipping this is a good first step but definitely just a first step,” one 22-year-old woman from Denver told me after the midterms.

“Most of the gains Gen Z has seen in the political atmosphere we’ve grown up amid [and] have come to know have been temporary and marginal,” a 21-year-old woman from New York City told me. “I think we have more cautious optimism about the trajectory of the [country], rather than millennials’ sheer optimism.”

Where the two generations come together perhaps is a shared recognition that anger, nihilism, and retreat are not the answer. In focus groups last spring, I started hearing fed-up young Americans tell me they were looking for candidates who offered a forward-looking vision on issues they care about — the economy, war, corruption, AI — rather than those who lament the opposite party or rationalize the status quo. And as the midterm cycle went on, it became clearer that they were over the culture war, and instead, interested in candidates who unite in service of fighting these core issues, rather than those who try to divide voters along identity or partisan lines.

It sounds cheesy for a generation that’s known for being so wildly jaded. But even jadedness gets old. And having grown up in a political ecosystem dominated by bullying, for Gen Z, a “politics of love” (as Talarico describes it) feels brand new. They may not be ready to let their guard down like their millennial forebears, but there’s a realization that hate, fear, and division haven’t gotten us very far.
