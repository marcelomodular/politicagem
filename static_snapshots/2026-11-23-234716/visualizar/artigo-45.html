It is truly wild that an executive with several reported affairs, a reputation for violently berating his staff, and a trail of deeply questionable deals could be invited back for a second go-round.

Then again, if President Donald Trump managed it…then why not his buddy, Jensen Huang?

Huang, the ex-ex-Nvidia CEO, is officially back at the helm of the giant chipmaker he cofounded 35 years ago — and all his past indiscretions have been forgiven, if still under investigation. And the stock market loves it, with investors joining Trump in betting that Huang’s singular business talents will make up for the considerable chaos he’s personally caused already.

Huang, you’ll recall, resigned less than two weeks ago following reports that he’d had an affair with a woman suspected of spying for China. Whether she actually was a spy is still an open question.

But other reporting has since cast lots of doubts on different aspects of Huang’s conduct and character. Like: reports that Nvidia, under Huang’s watch, may have indirectly sold restricted chips to China. Or that Huang allegedly told an employee he’d rip off his head and “pee on [his] brain,” among a number of other, er…inventive humiliations.

And yet! On Sunday, Nvidia’s board voted unanimously to reinstate him, apparently at the request of Trump himself. “I spoke to them. Let me just put it that way. And I convinced them,” Trump told CNBC in a Monday morning segment. Key to that decision: The president’s personal assurance that “the United States Government has no reason to believe that Jensen’s return to NVIDIA poses any risk to National Security.”

It’s an extraordinary turn in an extraordinary story that we very likely have not seen the end of just yet. Because the fight over Huang is also a fight over how America can continue dominating the high-stakes field of artificial intelligence.

Before the scandal, before the spying allegations, before all the drama…Huang was already a central player in the bitter debate over selling AI chips to China. The US restricts sales of some kinds of cutting-edge chips for fear they could help China develop more advanced AI. Huang has spent months telling Trump he should loosen those restrictions. (It’s not particularly difficult to imagine why.)

But as my colleague Andrew Prokop breaks down today, lawmakers, AI executives, and officials within Trump’s own administration are not sold. In fact, they’re deeply divided over whether loosening sales restrictions on AI chips would ultimately advance or erode America’s AI dominance.

In one camp are the so-called “China hawks,” who argue that selling powerful chips to China would very likely accelerate its AI development at America’s expense.

In the other camp are Nvidia and its allies in Trump’s orbit. They argue that America is better off selling China some chips — and keeping Chinese developers dependent on American technology — than abandoning that market to the Chinese competition.

This divide doesn’t map neatly onto party lines. While most prominent Democrats fall toward the hawkish end of the spectrum, the China question has divided Trump’s coalition.

Many GOP lawmakers and some of Trump’s top officials — including Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent — side more or less with Democrats on this. So does much of the national security establishment and the AI safety movement.

Meanwhile, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and former White House AI adviser David Sacks have made the case for keeping Chinese developers hooked on American chips. And Trump has increasingly favored their approach, growing generally more conciliatory toward China since his first term. Last year, his administration loosened restrictions on some Nvidia chip sales to China. (And last week Trump was, for what it’s worth, photographed holding Xi Jinping’s hand on an escalator.)

But Huang’s return won’t settle the China AI argument for long, regardless of Trump’s influence. When Democrats take control of Congress in January, they’ll gain the power to launch more rigorous investigations — including into Nvidia. And at least a few Republicans also say they have questions.

“Who in our government weighed in?” asked Sen. John Curtis (R-UT) in a post on X, referring to Huang’s reinstatement. “What did they tell the board? And on what basis did the board conclude there was no risk?”
