Título extraído: How to approach gifting so you don’t go broke, hurt anyone’s feelings, or end up drowning in crap

You don’t need me to tell you it’s expensive out there — groceries and gas cost a fortune, inflation is still hot hot hot, and getting a raise? Maybe next year, babe.

If this is relatable for you, you might be dreading the holidays. Maybe not the celebrations, exactly, but the gift giving of it all — all the spending, all the stuff. Between the cost to your bank account and the cost to the planet, I wouldn’t blame you if the thought of holiday gifting was causing some angst right now.

“The holidays are already stressful. Even if we think they’re fun, there’s a lot of stress associated with it,” financial therapist Lindsay Bryan-Podvin tells Vox. With higher prices on gas, groceries, travel, and eating out, “it’s compounding.”

It doesn’t have to be torturous, though. With the right approach, you can get your loved ones on board with a lower-cost, lower-waste holiday season. It just takes creativity and a little finesse.

Set your budget for gifts

So, you know you want to do things differently this season but you’re still planning to do some gifting. The first step? Make a budget, Bryan-Podvin says.

Once you get through the chaos of this season, think about setting up a budget for gifting in January or February.

Bryan-Podvin has a sinking fund called “Gifts” that she adds to every month. When a gifting occasion hits, she looks to that account to see how much she can afford to spend.

“Whatever’s in there helps direct how much I will or won’t spend,” she says.

To set this up, see if your savings account offers a “bucketing” feature — a way to subdivide your savings account by goal — or an option to open multiple savings accounts without fees or minimums. Then, allocate one bucket or savings account to gift giving, and set up a regular auto-deposit amount. You can always open a savings account with another bank, like Ally or Capital One, if your current bank doesn’t offer one of these options.

Here’s what she suggests: Decide what feels like a comfortable amount to spend — where you won’t feel anxious or resentful — and use that as your holiday budget. Don’t add up the cost of all the gifts you want to buy and then try to make that work with credit cards and buy-now-pay-later apps. You are the one making your budget — your budget is not being decided for you. Set that dollar amount, and then shop, craft, bake, scavenge, and forage within it.

If your gifting anxiety is more about consumerism, climate change, and the desire to give less stuff, now is also the time to think about an approach you would be comfortable with. Maybe you only want to give gifts to the kids in your life, or propose that your large extended family pull names from a hat instead of getting each person their own present. Perhaps your family would be excited to try an experience together, like going to a holiday light show and getting hot chocolate afterward, instead of exchanging presents.

Whatever strategy you land on, keeping a guiding principle in mind for your holiday spending will help you stay grounded when the Black Friday sales hit. It’s your budget and your holiday season — you get to decide what it looks like.

Set expectations with your friends and family

Culturally, many people feel that expectations around holiday gifting — not to mention traveling, hosting, and decorating — are rising, Bryan-Podvin says. “Gift giving has really expanded beyond giving within a family,” she says. “It seems like every group is doing a gift exchange, whether it’s your tennis club, your PTA group. … It might be well intentioned, but it’s also adding a lot of pressure.” The data backs that up: A new Gallup poll found that around a third of Americans still expect to spend more than $1,000 on holiday gifts in 2026, the highest figure recorded in the past 20 years.

But according to etiquette expert Lizzie Post, co-president at the Emily Post Institute, stepping back from the gift exchange — whether it’s within a social group or your family — is a perfectly acceptable thing to do. Don’t have the money this year? Don’t have the energy to plan, shop, and wrap? Anxious about adding to the landfill with unloved gifts? All it takes is a phone call to the person in charge to let them know.

“Try not to predict their reaction in [your] initial outreach,” she tells Vox. “Just say, ‘Hey, I’ve been so grateful that you’ve put this together each and every year, and John and I just aren’t going to be able to participate this year.’” That’s it — just be clear, candid, and grateful for the effort they’ve put in to organize the gift exchange. Nothing else is necessary. You can’t control someone else’s reaction, Post says.

Same goes for your family members, whether it’s a group thing or a one-on-one exchange. Just give each of them a call and be honest: “I won’t be able to do our gift exchange this year, but I can’t wait to jump back in down the road.” Post says she thinks hurt feelings are unlikely; most people understand times are tight right now.

If you’re choosing to give differently this year — only edible gifts, things you’ve crafted yourself, or secondhand items, for example — that’s also something you can share if it feels necessary. Just follow the same advice: Be clear, be confident, and don’t feel the need to apologize. And if you’re worried about the etiquette of declining to participate or participating in a different way, don’t: Post says it’s fine for traditions to change, evolve, or even pause over time.

“Not every year is going to be a year you can partake in something like that,” she says. “You don’t punish people for these moments, you work with them.”

How to feel really good about the gifts you’re giving

One important thing to keep in mind when you’re gathering presents this year: Gifting should feel good. Thinking about your loved ones, considering what they’ll actually like and use…that’s what it’s all about. Don’t let the targeted ads get in your head — there are many ways to give gifts that don’t involve spending a fortune on random novelty gifts or tchotchkes.

Megan Gray, senior shopping writer at Apartment Therapy, tells Vox she always makes a gift-giving list and checks it off, focusing on scoring deals and buying on sale rather than randomly buying things for people.

“Try to give a few minutes’ thought to each person,” she says, then write down what you’d like to get for them. “That always helps more than just going to the internet and searching. … That’s where you’re going to forget and put it off and then you’re going to have to do it last-minute, and that’s where you spend the money and get the gifts that aren’t going to resonate.”

To build your list, use one of these practical strategies to spend less money, generate less waste, and ultimately give more meaningful items.

Give subscriptions, memberships, or experiences

Subscriptions can be a meaningful way to give someone a year-long experience you know they’ll enjoy. For example, if you have a friend who loves to cook, give them a subscription to a recipe site like New York Times Cooking or America’s Test Kitchen. Have a sibling with kids? Give their family a membership to the local children’s museum, zoo, or science center. A radio-nerd bestie? Turn them into a This American Life partner for ad-free listening and bonus content. A cinema-loving partner? Gift them a year-long subscription to the Criterion Channel. Going this route also allows you to get more bang for your buck; a great board game or a family membership to a museum is likely going to be more meaningful and less expensive than buying each of your nieces and nephews a toy.

Like subscriptions and memberships, experiential gifts are a nice option for gift giving that doesn’t involve actual stuff (though they can be pricey, so you may want to avoid this if your budget is really tight). Event tickets and gift cards are a stellar idea here: sports, theater, concerts, live dance shows, Disney on Ice, cooking classes, luxury movie tickets with seat-side service, a wine tasting at the bar down the street, a stand-up comedy show — all are great ways to show you’ve thought about your loved one and their interests. And for a bonus gift? Offer to babysit if they have kids and you live nearby.

Give something they can consume

A bottle of wine, a case of nonalcoholic beer, a pie, a pound of your favorite coffee, a loaf of your finest sourdough…food and beverage gifts are a classic for a reason. If you’re a baker or maker, giving something homemade is a wonderful gesture, but if you’re not — don’t sweat it. Buying a thoughtful gift your friend or family member can consume is just as meaningful and will be appreciated.

For Gray, the shopping writer, practical items are a go-to gift idea. This can be especially smart when you’re participating in a large gift exchange, like a Secret Santa or similar.

“Sometimes my go-to is an umbrella,” she says. “You already have one? Here’s a second, keep it another bag, keep it in the car. I try to look for practical gifts people will use.”

Other practical gifts Gray recommends include glass food containers, a nice towel, or a piece of tech that’s multifunctional, like a charger that charges multiple items at once (like headphones, a smartwatch, and a phone).

Even if you’re not crafty, you can still give a homemade gift. Consider options like a personalized playlist, a nicely edited video of your favorite shared moments from the year, a jar of daily sentiments they can open and read whenever, or a framed photo of the two of you (or your group).

If you’re craft-curious, you could try making things like friendship bracelets, bookmarks, or simple ornaments. And if you have no creative skills to speak of, consider just writing a really heartfelt card expressing your gratitude and appreciation.

If you’re doing a gift exchange with a group — like a book club, your office mates, or the PTA — it may feel overwhelming to try to choose a gift for a person you don’t know that well. Instead, see if the group can agree on a “style” of gift that everyone enjoys and shop within that category. For instance, you could decide you’re all going to exchange cookbooks, favorite store-bought cookies, or beverages. You can also do this with loved ones: Commit to one category of gift and go all-in with it.

Another pro tip for next year

If you’re a parent, you know the dread of year-end spending — it adds up so fast. So next year, try and spread it out. This year, I personally kept an eye on my neighborhood “buy nothing” and “yard sale” groups on Facebook and gathered Christmas and birthday gifts for my son over time, and also bought new items I wanted to give him when they went on sale.

Now that we’re getting close to the holidays (and his December birthday) I’ve got pretty much all the pieces in place.

What to do if you blow your budget

Even the best planners and budgeters go a little wild sometimes. It’s okay — don’t beat yourself up.

Bryan-Podvin, the financial therapist, recommends looking at your spending from a place of curiosity, not judgment, and using it as information. For example, maybe you went home for the holidays for the first time in three years and got overly excited and bought an extra round at the bar or a fancier cheese plate than you might otherwise. Instead of fretting over the spending, think about the things you spent your money on and how they made you feel.

“Maybe making cookies was the most fun you had,” she says, but the fancy six-course, prix-fixe meal you had on New Year’s Eve was not good. “Okay well great, now you know — don’t do that next year.”

“Think about what felt good to spend and what didn’t feel good to spend, and then use that as information next year,” Bryan-Podvin adds. “The benefit of realizing you overspent in January is you can start to make some of those shifts and start doing things like putting $20 a paycheck away in a savings account.”
