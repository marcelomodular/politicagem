Título extraído: Will Iraqi Kurdistan Break Apart?

An escalating confrontation between the two constituent parts of Iraqi Kurdistan is threatening to tear the besieged region apart. Since 2006, the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan (PUK) and the Kurdistan Democratic Party (KDP), which fought a brutal civil war in the 1990s, have formed an uneasy alliance to govern the predominantly-Kurdish, autonomous region of northern Iraq. So far, the region and the alliance have survived a war with the Islamic State in 2014 and more than 1,000 attacks by Iran and its proxies since the start of 2026.

Now, however, a fight over the allotment of parliamentary seats and cabinet positions is pushing both parties toward a formal split. On Thursday, Nov. 5, the PUK formally withdrew from the region’s joint government. If the division worsens, it could damage the Iraqi Kurdistan's hard-won autonomy, threaten international support, and create new difficulties for citizens in their everyday lives.

On Thursday, Nov. 5, the PUK formally withdrew from the region’s joint government. If the division worsens, it could damage the Iraqi Kurdistan’s hard-won autonomy, threaten international support, and create new difficulties for citizens in their everyday lives.

On Nov. 11, U.S. Ambassador to Turkey Tom Barrack told the Kurdish parties that the Trump administration’s advice was to “get over it.” Barrack also spoke to Kurdish officials from both the PUK and KDP that week and underlined the importance of a united Kurdistan Region. In response, Bafel Talabani, the head of the PUK, called on the KDP to “get over it.” In the coming weeks, the parties’ willingness to compromise will determine if they can overcome their differences and prevent the breakup of the autonomous region.

Under U.S. mediation, the KDP and the PUK reached an agreement in 2006 whereby the KDP would control the presidency in Kurdistan and the PUK would control the Iraqi presidency in Baghdad. But this strategic agreement came under pressure after the PUK started to lose parliamentary seats to smaller opposition parties in 2009. In 2013, the KDP won 38 seats out of 100 in the Kurdish parliament compared to only 18 for the PUK. Then, in 2018, the KDP also attempted to challenge the PUK’s hold on the Iraqi presidency.

At the same time, the PUK also faced an internal power struggle after the death of its leader, Jalal Talabani, in 2017. Eventually, Talabani’s son Bafel Talabani took over the party in 2022, while his rival and cousin, Lahur Talabani, was subsequently given a five-year prison sentence for allegedly plotting an assassination, which he denies.

After elections in October 2024, the KDP again emerged as the largest party with 39 seats, as compared to 23 for the PUK. The KDP went on to argue that the allotment of cabinet roles and other government positions should reflect the parties’ respective seat shares, while the PUK maintained that they should be divided on a 50-50 basis, as stipulated in the 2006 agreement.

As a result of this disagreement, the Kurdistan Region has been without a functioning parliament since late 2024. On Nov. 4, however, the KDP managed to reach the legal quorum of 51 seats in order to convene the Kurdistan Region’s parliament. Having secured this majority with the help of defectors from opposition parties and three PUK members, the KDP proceeded to elect a new Presidency Board.

Hoshyar Siwaily, the head of the KDP’s foreign relations office, told Foreign Policy that over the past two years, the KDP and PUK have held intensive negotiations on forming a new government. However, he said that the negotiations between the two parties over the allocation of ministerial and senior government positions remained inconclusive, bringing the process to an impasse.

“The KDP was therefore compelled to form a parliamentary majority and reactivate the Kurdistan Parliament,” Siwaily said.

Saadi Ahmed Pira, a member of the PUK’s political bureau, disputed this assessment, claiming that while negotiations were still underway, and the deadlock was close to being solved, the KDP unilaterally decided to call for a session of parliament.

“The KDP was able to get the required number of MPs to meet the required 50+1 quorum by means of compensating several of our MPs and some MPs from other parties with large amounts of money, in essence bribing them to buy their votes,” Pira said.

The KDP denied the PUK’s allegations. “Almost all members of the other parties who attended the session categorically denied any wrongdoing, including allegations that they had received financial favors from the KDP,” Siwaily said.

In response to these developments, PUK leader Bafel Talabani said he was pulling out of the Kurdistan Regional Government (KRG) administration and threatened to form his own government outside of the KRG if the KDP moved ahead with its new parliamentary majority.

“You appoint a president of the region, we will appoint a president of the region. You appoint ministers, we will appoint ministers,” Talabani stated.

The KDP claims that it will do “everything in its power to prevent such a disastrous outcome.” On Nov. 9, KDP President Masoud Barzani called on the PUK to sit down with the KDP to end the political crisis in the region. So far, Talabani has publicly dismissed the statement as “not serious.”

Both parties will now look to Baghdad for support. On Nov. 9, KRG Prime Minister Masrour Barzani, a leader within the KDP, met with the Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi. After his visit, Barzani stated that Baghdad had agreed to continue paying all salaries in the region through the KRG administration. Bafel Talabani also traveled to Baghdad on Nov. 12 to lobby for his party and met with Iraqi leaders, including Iranian-backed Iraqi factions.

The PUK filed a lawsuit in Iraq’s top court in Baghdad on Nov. 10 to annul the reactivation of the parliament. On Nov. 15, the court rejected a separate request to suspend the parliament, and it will have a hearing on Dec. 14 on whether to reduce the KRG’s powers to a caretaker government.

Mohammed A. Salih, a nonresident senior fellow at the Foreign Policy Research Institute, told Foreign Policy that neither side wants to preclude reconciliation. “Both have shown considerable flexibility and pragmatism by keeping the door open for further negotiations and a negotiated settlement,” he said, adding, “The PUK’s withdrawal of its ministers from the cabinet is a blow to the KRG, but it will not bring the government down.”

Salih argued that a lasting break would run counter U.S. strategic interests in Iraq, particularly given ongoing concerns over Iranian influence. “Messaging from the U.S. side also suggests that Washington does not want to see the Kurdistan Region divided into two separate administrations,” he said.

If divisions persist, the price will also be paid by normal citizens. Binar Faeq Karim, an affiliate researcher at the think tank London Dialogue, suggested residents of the region would face “more complicated governance, difficulties in coordinating public services and economic policy and greater uncertainty over salaries, revenues and relations with Baghdad.”

Former U.S. Ambassador to Croatia Peter Galbraith, who has had a close relationship with the Kurds for more than four decades, told Foreign Policy that “recreating two administrations, as existed in the 1990s, would undermine all that has been achieved [by the Kurds] to date.”

“I understand bitter partisan differences—they exist in the U.S. as well—but it is sad when politicians allow them to threaten the very existence of Kurdistan,” Galbraith said.
