Título extraído: Vegan Thanksgiving recipes: 6 delicious alternatives to turkey

I haven’t eaten turkey in many years, and a large share of Americans who have it on Thanksgiving would rather eat something else, too. That turkey is the most overrated part of this glorious long weekend between the end of summer and the winter holidays has become a cultural bit as familiar as the dead bird itself. As the journalist Brian McManus wrote for Vice: “It is, almost without fail, a dried-out, depressing hunk of sun-baked papier-mâché — a jaw-tiringly chewy, unsatisfying, and depressingly bland workout.”

“Throw it in the garbage,” The Guardian commanded about the Thanksgiving turkey a decade ago. That’s indeed what happens to many of them — perhaps their culinary mediocrity is part of why the equivalent of well over 8 million turkeys are thrown away as food waste on Thanksgiving.

This is all very strange, and sad, too, because life is too short to eat bad food on holidays — and the Thanksgiving turkey isn’t a mere harmless tradition. I know it’s untoward and uncool to talk about where our meat comes from at the holiday table, but putting millions of birds through hell for a dish many people can’t stop complaining about seems like a particularly concerning situation.

Turkeys are factory-farmed in the second-largest numbers of any animal in the US, after chickens. Most spend their short lives packed by the thousands into crowded sheds, where the stress can drive them to attack one another; to prevent this, producers cut off parts of their beaks and toes without pain relief. The animals have been bred to grow so large, so fast — their weight has doubled since 1960 — that many struggle to even walk. Like other farmed animals, they don’t even reproduce naturally (more details of how that works on turkey farms, if you can stomach it, can be found here). The slaughter process is horrific.

I could go on, but surely that’s reason enough to try some different holiday traditions? I have lots of experience cooking and eating on Thanksgiving sans turkey in mixed company (that is, with both committed vegans and lifelong carnivores). Here are some of my favorite, satisfying, crowd-pleasing centerpieces to make instead:

Everyone was blown away by this plant-based version of a beef Wellington, which my made at last year’s Thanksgiving. It’s a variation on the New York Times’s vegetarian mushroom Wellington. The buttery (yes, even in the vegan version) puff pastry combined with the deep umami and sweet notes of several types of mushrooms and caramelized onions make it a gloriously rich main course that stands on its own and doesn’t feel like a plant-based imitation of something else.

The filling can take quite a bit of time to prepare, but certainly less than the ordeal required to make a turkey taste good. I love the port reduction that accompanies the New York Times version, but it can also be served with a mushroom gravy, a red wine and shallot gravy, or any other sauce you like. Also, if you’re concerned that this is not a very high-protein dish (mushrooms are extremely high in water and aren’t a high-protein food), I promise you’ll get enough protein from the other dishes on the table, and eating one meal without maximizing protein intake won’t do you any harm. But if you do want a protein-packed entrée, there are many options to consider! For this one, I may try adding beans or lentils to the filling in the future.

I’ve had wonderful experiences making this recipe on holidays. The cashew cream on top, combined with tahini and balsamic flavor in the lentils, make it impossible to stop eating. (Cashew cream, if you’ve never had it, is one of the greatest magic tricks of plant-based cooking.) This lentil shepherd’s pie is a similar concept that’s also excellent and great for those with nut allergies (it does contain sesame, though).

I couldn’t make this list without mentioning the option of stuffed squash or pumpkin, a beautiful classic of plant-based holiday fare. I haven’t made one of these in years, but there are endless recipe options online with hundreds of glowing reviews.

My family has been making these wonderfully firm, dense, chewy patties for many years. Developed by Isa Chandra Moskowitz, the undisputed queen of vegan cooking, the secret to their texture is wheat gluten, which has been used in Asian cuisines for centuries as a meat substitute. Gluten is one of the highest-protein things you can eat — I’m honestly not sure what distinguishes it from protein powder. Definitely serve these with gravy.

For a slightly non-traditional but still distinctly American detour from the usual Thanksgiving flavors, try these plant-based ribs, also made with gluten, by Miyoko Schinner (this same recipe is also found in Schinner’s cookbook, The Homemade Vegan Pantry). They were so good on the Fourth of July the past few years that we decided to turn them into a Thanksgiving main this year.

Super easy mode: Store-bought plant-based roasts

I know these can be polarizing, but they’ve gotten a lot better since the rubbery meat substitutes of yore. My favorite is Field Roast’s hazelnut and cranberry roast, which is wrapped in flaky puff pastry — kinda like a Wellington — followed by Gardein’s turk’y roast, which has a crispy breaded crust. There are plenty that I haven’t tried too: Juicy Marbles’s whole-cut loin is exceptionally well regarded, though pricey.

My own turkey alternative list is long: I haven’t even gotten to this tried-and-true lentil meatloaf or the creamy baked wild rice with carrots from Nisha Vora’s doorstopper Big Vegan Flavor. For most of us, there’s an extraordinary abundance of food to be grateful for on Thanksgiving — the least we could do is extend a little of that good fortune to turkeys.
