Título extraído: The Supreme Court is about to decide if assault rifle bans can exist

On December 2, the Supreme Court will hear two cases asking whether lawmakers may ban so-called assault rifles, semiautomatic weapons equipped with various features that allow a shooter to fire many rounds with greater accuracy than if they used an ordinary rifle.

The cases involve two similar laws, one out of Cook County, Illinois and another out of Connecticut, that seek to ban civilian ownership of AR-15s, a popular gun that was used in several recent mass shootings, and similar weapons. While there are subtle differences between the two laws, both ban semiautomatic rifles with features such as pistol grips that reduce recoil and make it easier for a shooter to rapidly fire many shots at a target.

(There is a chance that the Cook County case will be declared moot, but if that happens the Court will still be able to decide the Connecticut case.)

The most striking thing about the briefs in these two cases, Viramontes v. Cook County and Grant v. Higgins, is that they describe two entirely different Second Amendments that operate in completely different ways. Yet both of these competing Second Amendments can be found in this Court’s precedents.

It’s unclear just how much the Court’s decision to choose one Second Amendment over the other will matter to future cases, because the Court sometimes reaches results in gun rights cases that are difficult to justify under any of its previous precedents — the most consistent pattern in this Court’s Second Amendment decisions is that both Democratic and Republican justices typically reach the result that is preferred by their political party. But, at the very least, Viramontes and Grant give the justices an opportunity to bring coherence to one of the most disordered areas in US constitutional law.

The lawyers defending the assault rifle bans rely heavily on the Court’s decision in New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen (2022), a chaotic decision which held that all gun laws are unconstitutional unless the government can show that they fit within “this Nation’s historical tradition of firearm regulation.” Though Bruen is often criticized by the Court’s Democratic minority, the lawyers on the anti-assault rifle side of Viramontes and Grant argue that, for seven centuries, both English and American lawmakers have enacted laws that target weapons they deem “unusually dangerous,” and thus a ban on assault rifles fits well within this historical tradition.

The pro-gun side’s brief, meanwhile, engages with these historical arguments only as an afterthought. Instead, they primarily rely on a political compromise between Justice Antonin Scalia, the author of the Supreme Court’s landmark Second Amendment decision in District of Columbia v. Heller (2008), and Justice Anthony Kennedy, a more moderate conservative. According to the late Justice John Paul Stevens, Kennedy agreed to join Scalia’s opinion in Heller, but only if Scalia agreed to add some language limiting Heller’s impact and ensuring that many gun regulations would survive.

Ironically, the pro-gun side in Viramontes and Grant now argue that this compromise requires the modern-day Court to legalize assault rifles in all 50 states, even as the side arguing in favor of assault rifle bans rely on the broadly pro-gun framework announced in Bruen.

Realistically, it is likely that this Supreme Court, with its 6-3 Republican majority, will choose the path that the pro-gun side prefers. As that side notes in its brief, three current justices, including Justice Brett Kavanaugh, have previously suggested that assault rifle bans are unconstitutional. And the Court’s previous decisions applying Bruen often bend the rules to ensure that the pro-gun side wins.

But, if the Court does somehow decide to uphold these laws, it will be because Viramontes and Grant are the rare cases where Bruen cuts in favor of the pro-gun control side.

The two Second Amendments, explained

The Second Amendment states that “A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed,” and up until fairly recently, the Supreme Court took the first thirteen words of this amendment very seriously. As the Court said in United States v. Miller (1939), the “obvious purpose” of the Second Amendment was to “render possible the effectiveness” of militias, and the amendment must be “interpreted and applied with that end in view.”

The Court abandoned this textualist approach to gun rights, however, in District of Columbia v. Heller (2008), which held that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to possess a gun for personal “self-defense.” The bulk of the Heller opinion is a complicated originalist argument that, despite the amendment’s words, its actual purpose is to “guarantee the individual right to possess and carry weapons in case of confrontation.”

But Heller also includes three anomalous paragraphs — Section III of the opinion — which enumerate several types of gun regulation that are not prohibited by the Second Amendment. The Court declares, for example, that laws forbidding “possession of firearms by felons and the mentally ill,” or “the carrying of firearms in sensitive places such as schools and government buildings,” or “prohibiting the carrying of ‘dangerous and unusual weapons,’” are all consistent with Heller. (As I’ll get into below, this last line, the one about “dangerous and unusual” weapons, plays a starring role in the pro-gun side’s brief in Viramontes and Grant.)

Shortly before his death in 2019, retired Justice Stevens revealed some of the internal deliberations that led Scalia to insert this list of exceptions to the Second Amendment. According to Stevens, then-Justice Kennedy asked Scalia to make “some important changes” to the Heller opinion after Scalia’s original draft was circulated to the full Court — and, because the non-Kennedy justices split 4-4 in Heller, Scalia had to accommodate Kennedy if he wanted to remain in the majority.

And so Scalia added Section III of the Heller opinion as a political compromise to secure Kennedy’s fifth vote.

This compromise could have died in 2022, when the Court handed down Bruen. Bruen, after all, held that judges should determine the meaning of the Second Amendment by asking whether a modern day law fits within a “historical tradition of firearm regulation.” Under this test, government lawyers must show that the modern-day gun law they are defending is sufficiently similar to “analogous regulations” that existed when the Constitution was framed.

That’s obviously a different legal rule than the one laid out in Section III of Heller, which turned on a private negotiation between Scalia and Kennedy.

But Kennedy’s successor, Justice Brett Kavanaugh, wrote a separate concurring opinion in Bruen that explicitly endorsed the Heller compromise — Kavanaugh even blockquoted most of Section III of Heller in that concurring opinion. And Kavanaugh’s concurring opinion was joined by Chief Justice John Roberts. So the Heller compromise is still alive today because, even on a 6-3 Republican Supreme Court, two members of the majority want to keep that compromise alive.

For Second Amendment litigators, this means that gun rights cases often have to proceed on two different tracks. If a gun rights plaintiff challenges a gun law that doesn’t fit within one of the Heller’s enumerated exceptions to the Second Amendment, then judges can resolve the case by using the historical analysis demanded by Bruen (an analysis, it’s worth noting, that nearly always results in the law being struck down — at least if the case reaches the Supreme Court). But if a gun law does fit within one of Heller’s exceptions, Roberts and Kavanaugh may potentially form a majority with the Court’s three Democrats to uphold the law.

What makes Viramontes and Grant, the assault rifle cases, unusual is that the two sides of the case have flipped on whether Bruen or Section III of Heller should apply. The side that wants legal assault rifles in every state leans hard into Heller’s line about “dangerous and unusual weapons,” claiming that assault rifles are actually very common in the United States and therefore not “unusual” at all. At one point in their brief, they claim that “approximately 26 million people” currently own an AR-15 or similar gun.

Meanwhile, the lawyers supporting assault rifle bans cite multiple historical examples of English and early American laws that banned commonly used weapons after lawmakers determined that those weapons are too dangerous to be widely available. These lawyers, in other words, essentially ask the Court to look past the Heller compromise and apply the (ordinarily very pro-gun) framework laid out in Bruen.

The briefs defending the assault rifle bans are extraordinarily persuasive, and they have almost no chance of winning five votes on this Supreme Court

Connecticut’s brief defending its assault rifle ban opens with a harrowing reminder of why the state passed this law — in 2012, “a lone gunman armed with an AR-15-style rifle murdered twenty first-graders and six educators inside Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Connecticut.” It’s clear that Connecticut’s leaders care a great deal about preserving this law. Among other things, they hired former US Solicitor General Elizabeth Prelogar — in my opinion the single best Supreme Court advocate in the country right now — as their lead counsel.

Prelogar is also the only lawyer to successfully defend a gun law before the Supreme Court after Bruen was handed down. In United States v. Rahimi (2024), she persuaded the Court to uphold a federal law that disarms people who are subject to domestic violence restraining orders.

Her brief in Grant lays out a long list of old laws banning a particular weapon that was already in common use when they were banned. The oldest law she cites is a 1396 English law banning launcegays, “light, ashen lances with sharp points at both ends,” that were the “favored ‘instrument[s] of lawlessness and murder;” in fourteenth century England.

Prelogar’s brief also cites a 16th-century ban on crossbows and concealable firearms known as “pocket dagges,” and a 17th-century ban on concealable daggers known as “steelets.” Additionally, she cites early American laws banning bowie knives, concealable handguns, and “slungshots” — a piece of rope or leather tied around a heavy weight to create a bludgeoning weapon.

These examples, she argues, demonstrate that “this Nation’s historical tradition of firearm regulation” includes laws banning weapons that lawmakers determined are too dangerous after those weapons entered into common use. A ban on assault rifles fits into this tradition, as lawmakers in many jurisdictions decided to ban them after they became a preferred weapon for mass shooters. According to Prelogar’s brief, “since 2012, AR-15s or similar rifles were gunmen’s chosen weapon in twelve of the seventeen deadliest mass killings in the United States.”

It’s a solid argument, but it’s also unlikely to persuade this Supreme Court. For starters, recall that Kavanaugh, who endorsed the Heller compromise in Bruen, is one of the more moderate members of the Court’s Republican bloc in gun cases. But Kavanaugh endorsed the pro-assault rifle lawyers’ argument that bans on AR-15s are unconstitutional because these weapons are “in common use today” in a 2011 dissenting opinion that he wrote as a lower court judge.

Without Kavanaugh, Prelogar and her team need to hold onto Roberts and also find one other vote among the four remaining Republican justices. That’s a tough task.

And even if Prelogar does convince the Court to apply the Bruen framework, and not the Heller compromise with its line about dangerous and unusual weapons, some of the Court’s post-Bruen decisions suggest that the Republican justices are not applying its historical analysis in good faith.

Most notably, in Wolford v. Lopez (2026), the Court struck down a Hawaii law that required gun owners to get explicit permission from a private business’s owner or manager before they can bring a gun onto that businesses’ property. It was an unusual law — at least in modern-day America — but it appears that Hawaii’s legislature wrote the law this way specifically so it would survive a Bruen challenge. Four US states had similar laws around the time when the Second Amendment was crafted.

This Court, in other words, is perfectly willing to strike down gun laws that a majority of the justices do not like, even when those laws do align with “this Nation’s historical tradition of firearm regulation.” And, in Viramontes and Grant, they don’t even have to come up with some tenacious historical analysis in order to strike down assault rifle bans. They can simply quote the Heller compromise’s use of the word “unusual” and be done with it.
