Título extraído: To combat sexual assault on campus, it’s time to look past Title IX

In the weeks since reports about an alleged gang rape at Cornell went public, I’ve heard a lot of people ask why we haven’t made more progress on campus sexual assault.

I understand the question. I started working on the issue as a student activist more than 15 years ago. Now, as an attorney at Public Justice, I represent student survivors of sexual assault in lawsuits against their schools. Seeing the stories out of Cornell sometimes makes me feel like nothing has changed since I was an undergraduate.

But that’s not quite right: We did make progress. Schools started taking more seriously their responsibilities to address sexual assaults, providing survivors with academic and mental health support to help them stay in school and setting up procedures to investigate allegations. This progress was spurred significantly by enforcement of Title IX, the federal law that prohibits sex discrimination in education and offers protections to student survivors of sexual assault.

However, that progress was met by backlash — both political backlash specific to Title IX and a broader hostility to civil rights in the federal courts. As a result, Title IX is now less effective in protecting student survivors than it was a decade ago. And in some ways, Title IX now effectively encourages schools to mistreat victims. What a bitter irony for a sex discrimination law.

Some of these changes, such as the Trump administration’s rewriting of Title IX’s regulations, have received plenty of public attention. But others have flown under the radar, including a 2022 Supreme Court decision that reduced the damages available in Title IX lawsuits, as well as the rise of “reverse discrimination” lawsuits by men accused of sexual harassment.

Despite the lack of attention, their effects on students have been significant. But this doesn’t mean we have to resign ourselves to a steady unwinding of progress. There are other routes survivors can take to hold schools accountable for tolerating sexual violence on campus.

Donald Trump twisted Title IX around

Since President Richard Nixon signed Title IX into law in 1972, federal agencies have enforced the sex discrimination law against schools. Title IX is perhaps best known for ushering in a revolution in women’s sports, but addresses a wide range of forms of sex discrimination, including pregnancy discrimination, sex-segregated classrooms, and sexual harassment. Starting in the 1990s, the Education Department began publishing guidance documents that outlined schools’ Title IX responsibilities to address sexual harassment, including sexual assault. These covered topics like definitions of sexual harassment and how schools are supposed to respond to reports, mostly leaving the minutiae of schools’ investigatory and disciplinary procedures to their own discretion.

Under President Barack Obama, the US Department of Education issued a number of high-profile guidances and ramped up enforcement efforts, with the goal of pushing schools to respect victims’ Title IX rights, which many had long ignored. Those developments were lauded by student activists and civil rights groups. But critics on the right, and some on the left, saw these changes as going too far to protect survivors at the cost of due process of the accused.

In 2020, the first Trump administration put its own, very different, stamp on Title IX. It promulgated a new set of Title IX regulations that govern how the Department of Education analyzes administrative complaints it receives through its Office for Civil Rights. President Joe Biden tried to replace these regulations in 2024, but two courts struck them down early the next year. So Trump’s 2020 regulations are back in effect today.

The 2020 regulations are most known for the procedural requirements they require of schools investigating sexual harassment — requirements that are often understood to protect accused students, though they are so complicated that they might chiefly benefit whoever can afford to pay an attorney.

At colleges and universities, the regulations require schools to use a single onerous and adversarial hearing model in which the parties’ representatives directly cross-examine the witnesses, including the survivor. Some students choose a lawyer as their representative, but some pick a parent or friend. As a result, at Cornell, Jane Doe could have been directly cross-examined by one of the accused men’s dads or fraternity brothers. Understandably, this process can be retraumatizing for survivors. Some refuse to pursue an investigation as a result.

That’s a real shame, because there are other truth-seeking options that schools use in other kinds of disciplinary matters. In imposing this one-size-fits-all model, the rules forbid schools from using hearing models that courts have blessed as constitutional, and which reduce face-to-face conflict by routing the parties’ questions through neutral third parties.

There is more to the 2020 regulations than process, though. Less reported are the changes they made to Title IX’s liability standard — that is, what kinds of harassment schools have to address and how good of a job schools are expected to do. Under previous administrations, Democratic and Republican alike, the Department of Education interpreted Title IX to require schools to respond to severe or pervasive harassment, and to do so promptly and effectively. The Trump regulations instead require schools only to address severe and pervasive harassment, and to respond in a way that is not “deliberately indifferent” or “clearly unreasonable.”

That means that schools are allowed, under current regulations, to ignore reported sexual harassment until it escalates and to then treat the victim unreasonably — so long as that response isn’t clearly unreasonable. In practice, that might mean that the Department of Education will tolerate a school dragging its feet to investigate, or failing to provide a survivor the academic accommodations she needs after a sexual assault, so long as the Department believes those failures were merely unreasonable and not “clearly” so.

Trump justified the 2020 regulations as necessary to protect the accused, but the most significant provisions instead protect schools like Cornell from liability for mistreating survivors. Schools have less reason to fear Title IX complaints filed against them by victims with the Department of Education because, when it comes to doing right by survivors, the federal government expects less of schools now.

The Supreme Court also changed the landscape

In addition to reporting to the Department of Education, students can also file Title IX lawsuits against their schools in court. A 2022 Supreme Court decision, however, has made it much harder, and less effective, for survivors to do so.

On its face, Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller didn’t look like it had much to do with campus rape. The case concerned disability discrimination that Jane Cummings, who is deaf and legally blind, faced when she sought physical therapy from the defendant. The Supreme Court took up the case to consider the Fifth Circuit’s decision that Cummings could not receive emotional distress damages in the case.

The appeals courts’ reasoning went like this: The statutes under which Cummings brought her case were “spending clause” statutes — statutes that condition the receipt of federal funding on certain conditions, such as an agreement not to discriminate. The Supreme Court has previously said that spending clause statutes can be analogized to contracts. So, the Fifth Circuit reasoned, Cummings could not receive emotional distress damages because such damages are usually not available in contract disputes (though sometimes they are).

This Fifth Circuit’s decision was a break from longstanding practices from courts across the country, which all permitted emotional distress damages in spending clause cases. The appeals court seemed so obviously wrong that even Biden’s solicitor general encouraged the Supreme Court to take up the case — and progressive lawyers are generally not in the business of asking this conservative Supreme Court to weigh in on civil rights law.

The general rule proved right in this case. The Supreme Court held that the Fifth Circuit’s outlier rule was correct. As a result, nationwide, plaintiffs can no longer receive emotional distress damages in spending clause cases.

And Title IX is a spending clause statute. So, although Cummings did not arise from a Title IX case, courts have near-unanimously applied its reasoning to Title IX.

Emotional distress damages had long made up the bulk of money damages for students in anti-discrimination lawsuits. A workplace discrimination plaintiff may be able to show that they lost out on years of pay because their employer wrongly fired them; student plaintiffs usually cannot point to such concrete economic injuries. They could, however, show that they had suffered significant emotional distress — at least until Cummings.

Now, Title IX cases result in much smaller jury verdicts and settlements. That, on its own, makes the prospect of a Title IX lawsuit by a survivor much less worrisome to a school: If the school is sued, it will be on the hook for far less than before.

And the chance of any lawsuit at all has diminished as well. With a smaller potential monetary award on the other side, fewer attorneys are willing to take on these cases. Most plaintiff-side anti-discrimination lawyers work on contingency, meaning they take a portion of an eventual award rather than charging up front, which the vast majority of students could not afford.

Because the expected money value of Title IX cases has gone down, lawyers know their contingency fee will go down, too — meaning more attorneys are just saying no. For students, too, the stress of a lawsuit may just not be worth the sum they can expect if they win. All this, too, makes a school less worried about a mistreated survivor filing a Title IX suit.

How men started using Title IX in their favor

One kind of Title IX lawsuit, however, has been on the rise for the last decade: Lawsuits brought by male students and employees who say they were disciplined for sexual harassment by their schools as a result of unlawful anti-male bias.

If a student or teacher believes they have been wrongly disciplined — for sexual harassment or anything else — they often have a range of legal tools available. Depending on the school, they may be able to file a challenge claiming procedural injuries in violation of constitutional due process, or a contract-law violation of the school’s own rules. One of the men accused in the Cornell case, for example, filed a lawsuit earlier this year alleging that the school violated state law by failing, in his view, to notify him of the charge against him. (He was charged with sexual exploitation but found responsible for attempted sexual exploitation.) His lawsuit was dismissed by a judge and is now on appeal.

Over the last decade or so, however, courts have been unusually receptive to a different litigation strategy: Male students and employees — mostly professors — say their school punished them because they are men, and so engaged in sex discrimination in violation of Title IX. As colleagues and I explained in a law review article, these cases rarely present the kind of evidence that courts generally require in anti-discrimination cases. For example, they often rely on perceived procedural deficiencies as evidence of a school’s animus against men. But in cases brought by women and people of color, courts have said that such procedural deficiencies, on their own, don’t give rise to an inference of discrimination; to make out an anti-discrimination claim, plaintiffs must also show that those procedural deficiencies are the result of a discriminatory motive. (Case law is especially clear that a student victim can’t bring a Title IX lawsuit based on their school’s violation of its own procedures.) These anti-male bias suits also often rely on general allegations that schools are under pressure to respond to campus sexual assault, without tying that pressure to anti-male bias specifically.

Nonetheless, many courts have allowed these lawsuits to proceed. These results reflect political backlash to Title IX and Me Too more than established legal doctrine. And the Second Circuit — the federal appeals court that oversees cases out of New York, where Cornell is based — may be the jurisdiction friendliest to these anti-male bias plaintiffs, overruling trial courts that have dismissed them. (Cummings is less of an issue for these plaintiffs because they are often seeking reinstatement or expunged disciplinary records rather than money damages alone.)

The result is that schools today have reason to be more afraid of a Title IX lawsuit from a male student they suspend for sexual assault than of a Title IX lawsuit by his victim.

No one invites me into university general counsels’ offices, but, from talking to practitioners across the country, I feel confident that these legal incentives shape how schools respond to sexual assault allegations — including how they make disciplinary decisions.

What tools can we use instead?

Title IX is a less effective tool for student survivors than it was a decade ago. But it is not the only tool available. Jane Doe’s case against Cornell doesn’t include a Title IX claim. Instead, she is using state law, including the New York State Human Rights Law. That statute isn’t limited by Cummings or other bad federal precedent. Not every state, though, has laws as good as New York’s; the benefit of a federal law is that it doesn’t leave students at the whims of local legislators.

Those students, though, can still benefit from extra-legal tools. When I was a student, we knew that schools weren’t only responsive to legal risk. They also made decisions to protect their reputations. Cornell probably cares more right now about public outrage than it cares about what it may owe Jane Doe if she wins her lawsuit.

Building and sustaining public pressure on schools to do right by survivors will take coordinated student organizing on and across campuses. I know, from my experience as a campus organizer many years ago, that it is hard, and that it will invite backlash. But my hope is this generation will be more successful than we were.
