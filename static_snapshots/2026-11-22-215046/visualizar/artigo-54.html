Remember two weeks ago, when Vice President JD Vance was having a bad week? It’s gotten much, much worse.

Vance has spent most of the last two years as the MAGA movement’s heir apparent — so much so that Secretary of State Marco Rubio, potentially his top rival for the 2028 GOP presidential nomination, told a reporter last year that “If JD Vance runs for president, he’s going to be our nominee, and I’ll be one of the first people to support him.”

Suffice to say, that no longer looks like the case: Vance, more than any other potential Republican candidate in 2028, emerged from the midterms in bad political shape, and President Donald Trump has been putting him through hell ever since.

Specifically, Vance has found himself in a trap of his own making on two issues: The Iran war and immigration. In a long social media post the day after the midterms, he blamed the war for his party’s electoral catastrophe; more recently, he described “bipartisan immigration reform” that would allow undocumented immigrants to stay in the country as “morally and electorally disastrous.”

The problem with those two stances is that he’s still Trump’s vice president, and Trump refuses to admit that the Iran war was the wrong decision (though he’s been willing enough to concede that it was an electoral anchor). Trump is also in the midst of showing occasional, vague interest in the aforementioned bipartisan immigration reform. And he’s reportedly not happy with Vance’s subtweets.

Both of those tensions were on full display Friday in a press conference by Trump, Vance, and Rubio, where Vance dodged a question on whether the war was worth the political fallout — an area where Trump has strongly suggested he’d like more backup from his second-in-command. “The president said he would do it again,” Vance told reporters. “My view is that my goal in my job is to support the president of the United States.”

That answer, you might correctly surmise, is almost certainly not going to do it with Trump over the long run. (This being the same president so bent on conforming the world around him to his views that he dispenses matching too-big shoes to his team, and they wear them.)

Vance’s plight is particularly dire because he doesn’t really have anywhere to run. His options look something like this:

  1. Hug Trump, hope for an endorsement, and see if the president’s reflected goodwill can push him to the nomination (and potentially to a general election wipeout, if Trump’s trajectory with voters stays the same);
  2. Break from Trump more dramatically, forfeiting an endorsement and MAGA enthusiasm, and try to stake out his own brand in the primary and the general;
  3. Or keep on keeping on, cryptically sniping at opponents from social media until Trump gets mad anyway.

Meanwhile, the weaker Vance looks, the more his potential rivals are likely to eye the throne for themselves. In a poll released Thursday by Emerson College, Vance’s potential 2028 support has collapsed from 54 percent this time last year to just 20 percent, while Rubio’s has jumped from 6 percent to 36 percent (more than any other polled candidate, with Vance in second). And Rubio, according to CNN, is leaving some people with the impression that he’s more open to a run.

Other Republicans, including outgoing Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis and Texas Sen. Ted Cruz, are also believed to be considering runs, and both would have the advantage of being able to run from outside a Trump administration that seems unlikely to grow more popular over the next two years.

Vance, in other words, already looks like he could be approaching his best-by date — and he still has a very long year-plus until the first votes are cast in the 2028 primary.
