Americans have debated, derided, praised, and protested the frontier AI labs. But they’ve never been able to directly own a piece of one…until tomorrow at 9 am.

That’s when Anthropic, developer of the chatbot Claude, is expected to go public at a valuation of more than $1 trillion. That would immediately rank the almost-six-year-old lab among the world’s most valuable publicly traded companies — up there with the likes of Walmart and JPMorgan Chase.

It’s a major public test of what investors think Anthropic is worth, at a moment when a lot of people — policymakers, regulators, and technologists among them — are questioning the economic and social value of the AI industry. AI stocks have been shaky lately, and many of the new congressional Democrats have promised to take a tougher stance toward data centers and related technology.

But you wouldn’t know it from the hype around the IPO, which is on track to become one of the largest ever. Anthropic initially set a price range of $70 to $78 a share, then reportedly revised the offering at $83 per share as demand surged far beyond the number of shares available.

Investors are apparently betting that Anthropic has more growing to do. For ordinary buyers, that represents a chance to share in a company whose technology could cost us our jobs…if not our very existence on Earth. 💀

The biggest winners, however, will almost undoubtedly be the executives, employees, and early investors at Anthropic itself. The timing of the IPO helps with that.

Some of America’s most iconic companies went public very early in their lifecycles. (Think Apple, Amazon, and eBay, which all IPO-ed before they were five years old.)

But over the past two decades or so, many of America’s biggest businesses have instead grown in private. Between the early 2000s and 2025, the median age of a company at IPO doubled from six years in 2000 to 12 in 2025.

Analysts credit two main factors for these increasingly late-breaking IPOs. One is the fast-growing availability of private capital from venture funds, wealthy individuals, and other companies. Firms that once needed to go public to raise money to grow can now raise that money privately.

The other factor is the desire to maintain control over the company and its information: Private firms don’t have to file public financial reports or give public shareholders a vote on corporate matters.

But while that may be good for companies and their leaders, it’s not always or unambiguously great for the general public. Financial reports shed valuable light on how a business operates and where its risks lie, for instance.

There’s a financial downside for us normies, too. “The way I see it, the later a company goes public, the more of its growth gains accrue to private investors,” Brad Badertscher, a professor of accountancy at the University of Notre Dame who has studied IPO trends, said in an email to Vox.

Under this system, insiders like executives, employees, and early investors can nab shares at a low price while the company is still young and then benefit if its value goes up. Retail investors only join the party after much of that growth has already happened.

Of course, growth is never guaranteed. Early investors, private or public, also take on a lot of risk. And even now, several years in, new investors are still taking a substantial risk when they buy shares in Anthropic — a company that, in the grand scheme of recent IPOs, isn’t even that late to the process.

There’s the threat of greater regulation from a Democratic Congress. The fallout of the shake-up at Nvidia. The specter of a potential AI market crash (to say nothing about the aforementioned end of the world).

If would-be shareholders are bothered, however…you’d never know it! They’ve been waiting years to buy Anthropic stock, and they very much want in.
