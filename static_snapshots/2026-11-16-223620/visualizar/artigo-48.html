Título extraído: What the results of the 2026 midterms taught us about the next election

Everyone from party insiders to the pundits have now parsed the results from the midterm elections, and few races are left to call. Soon, the political world will turn its sights to the 2028 presidential election — but first, America, Actually is taking a moment to reflect on eight months of reporting across the country.

That included anger about the construction of a data center in southern New Jersey; Democratic hopes for a governing trifecta in Wisconsin, understanding the “Trump slump” — a combination of the pain from tariffs and the fear of immigration enforcement — in Las Vegas; and hearing voters in a crucial swing district in Pennsylvania connect the war in Iran with high gas prices.

No one votes for president in 2027, but the issues that we saw on the road are a reminder that politics doesn’t only live in the voting booth (and, of course, they’ll have implications for 2028).

This week on America, Actually, I spoke with host Astead Herndon about the moments that we just can’t stop thinking about from our reporting.

Below is an excerpt from that conversation, focusing on two aspects of this year’s midterms: how the results could reverberate in the GOP presidential race and what Angie Nixon’s close loss in her Senate race in Florida says about who the Democratic Party establishment will choose to support as the 2028 presidential primary gets underway in 2027. It has been edited for length and clarity.

There’s much more in the full show, including a discussion of Trump’s declining cultural cachet, an evolving Democratic base, and the rise of the lefty boomer resistance. You can listen to full episodes of America, Actually wherever you get your podcasts or watch on Vox’s YouTube channel.

We reported that there were really two strategies that Republicans were using going into this year’s midterm. The first one was basically ignore Trump or distance yourself from Trump, and the other one was to throw money at the polling problem.

Now, obviously, neither of those worked. I don’t really want to flatten that analysis too much, but there are plenty of examples of those who had actually distanced themselves from Trump who ended up losing, right? I’m thinking about Mike Lawler from New York. Obviously, Vivek Ramaswamy in his Ohio governor’s race crashed and burned. We’ve had a lot of really high-profile races where going away from Trump didn’t really work.

One foot in and one foot out doesn’t help when something is this universally disliked.

And also, the Republican Party didn’t use the levers of power at any point to push back against what Trump has been doing in the last couple years. I think the Iran war is the big one. Like, it was time to shine and to say something, and they didn’t.

But what does that mean for 2027? Is the Trump era over?

I don’t like that question because at the same time, we’ve seen him use executive power. I don’t think he’s going to back off of his agenda. Steven Miller’s the new chief of staff. Obviously, that represents a double down in itself. But it’s up to Congress to do its job to reassert itself, right?

Which they have abdicated completely.

Yeah, they’ve abdicated on their own. And so I think it remains to be seen how they even go about the work of holding [Trump] accountable.

The other piece is that Trump remains the most popular figure within the Republican Party. And the one thing we know about Republicans is that they circle around him as he’s embattled. And so I just don’t think we can assume that even a mass rejection from the public now means he does not have the most important role in choosing their nominee for the future. I think that’s going to be a big question that 2027 plays out.

His popularity generally is waning. His popularity specifically with them is intact. Does that change?

Do you think that the distancing or playing the blame game that we’re seeing from Vice President JD Vance or Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis, does that signal anything for 2027, or it’s too early?

It means that Vance has some challengers. It means DeSantis is probably running, and Ted Cruz and all these others.

And I do think that that coronation that might have seemed like a cakewalk three weeks ago is less so now. But I think we’ve got some more twists and turns to come.

My last one, honestly, is an answer I’ve thought about a lot when we talked to Angie Nixon, the Senate Democratic candidate in Florida, and it was actually when I asked her about her joining the Democratic Socialists of America, that became such a way that national politics was defining her race.

And one of the things I remember is the way that she shrugged off the idea that she should be defined by that label. and I think it really came through in that interview that this was someone who had their own political brand, that was kind of marching to the beat of their own drum, and that was what drove that primary win in Florida.

I think this matters a lot for 2027: The gap between how national politics and our kind of media ecosystem sees these fights, particularly between moderate, progressive, DSA, the center, is not how the country’s engaging.

The fuzziness is so important, and I think a candidate like that — who now data’s telling us won counties like Miami-Dade [in Florida] — I think speaks to the idea that people are ingesting individuals and their ideas rather than the labels that sometimes we put them in.

The other thing I was thinking about when thinking about Angie Nixon — obviously there’s been a huge discussion about whether the Democratic Party missed a chance to invest in her race and win in Florida.

I think we’re entering a period where those types of insiders’ decisions matter a lot. When we talk about the 2027 presidential race, there’ll be no primaries decided. This will be a lot of polling. This will be a lot of donor decisions. This will be a lot of community group endorsements and access, handshaking, and stuff like that.

And so sometimes those presumptions of who’s electable and who’s not may not actually matter to the public but they matter in terms of shaping who gets to Iowa, shaping who gets to New Hampshire.

I think that’s a huge thing for next year. Those kinds of insider discussions about who can win and who not, or what they presume the public wants or not — I don’t think that actually changes people’s votes. I think that crowd has kind of had a credibility loss with the Democratic base. But I do think it can shape some of the early markers that help a candidate rise and fall in the next six to eight months.
