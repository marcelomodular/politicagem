Título extraído: The Supreme Court is about to decide whether to gut countless federal laws

Nearly five decades ago, in Cannon v. University v. Chicago (1979), the Supreme Court held that Title IX, the federal law forbidding most schools from engaging in sex discrimination, could be enforced by private plaintiffs filing individual lawsuits against those schools. It was an important decision, because without it, Title IX would be a much weaker law that would only function when the federal government charges someone with violating it.

The Court has since reaffirmed its decision in Cannon at least four times, provided that the plaintiff alleges intentional discrimination. And yet, on November 30, the Supreme Court will hear Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that asks whether Title IX’s prohibition on sex discrimination may be enforced by through such individual lawsuits.

The fact that this Court took up a legal question that the Supreme Court has already answered many times is an ominous sign for victims of sex discrimination — and potentially for millions of other individuals whose rights to education, healthcare, and numerous other essentials are protected by federal law. The best case scenario for civil rights plaintiffs is that the justices simply hand down a sixth decision reaffirming a longstanding right, and the worst case scenario could render countless federal laws unenforceable.

Crowther involves a highly technical, but extremely important legal concept known as “implied causes of action.” Not all federal laws may be enforced by private litigants in court — sometimes, for example, only the federal government itself is allowed to sue in order to enforce a particular law. Some statutes, meanwhile, contain an explicit provision stating that private individuals may bring lawsuits to enforce them.

Other laws, including Title IX, do not explicitly authorize suits. But the Supreme Court nonetheless concluded in Cannon and in several other decisions that a right to sue is implicit in Title IX’s text — that is, it contains an “implied cause of action.” Until recently, the Court’s decision in Gonzaga University v. Doe (2002) laid out the rules that judges were supposed to use in order to determine whether a particular federal law contains an implied cause of action, and therefore may be enforced by private plaintiffs.

But the rules governing implied causes of action grew muddled a little over a year ago, when the Court’s decision in Medina v. Planned Parenthood (2025) seemed to abandon Gonzaga. They grew even more muddled in June, when the Court handed down yet another decision reaffirming Gonzaga. So it’s now impossible even for veteran civil rights lawyers to determine which laws may be enforced through individual lawsuits.

In any event, Crowther gives the Court a prime opportunity to resolve this confusion, and clarify which laws may actually be enforced by private litigants. The stakes in this case, moreover, are absolutely enormous, as a too-stingy approach to implied causes of action would deactivate a wide array of federal laws.

Many of the Court’s most important implied causes of action cases, for example, involve Medicaid — a federal program that provides healthcare to nearly 73 million people. If federal Medicaid law — which doesn’t just provide health coverage to low-income patients but also lays out a long list of rights those patients enjoy — becomes unenforceable, millions of lives will be jeopardized.

These cases often go to the heart of Americans’ ability to care for themselves and their loved ones. In Health and Hospital Corp. v. Talevski (2023), the Court held (among other things) that a federal law that prohibits nursing homes from unnecessarily drugging their patients into a stupor is enforceable. In Medina, the Republican justices effectively repealed a federal law that allows Medicaid patients to choose their own health provider.

Crowther, in other words, could be one of the most consequential — and one of the cruelest — decisions the Court hands down during its current term, because a decision for the defendants in Crowther could effectively turn countless lifesaving federal laws into a nullity.

What are the rules governing implied causes of action?

One frustrating thing about this area of the law is that the justices frequently change the rules governing implied causes of action. In the 1960s the Court took an expansive approach to implied causes of action, but it spent much of the later two decades of the 20th century limiting which federal laws may be enforced by private lawsuits.

Still, this area of the law appeared to stabilize when the Court handed down Gonzaga, which held that “for a statute to create private rights [to sue], its text must be phrased in terms of the persons benefited.”

Thus, for example, if Congress passes a law stating that “no dirty person shall be denied access to a bath,” this law would be enforceable through private lawsuits because the law is phrased in terms of who benefits from it (dirty people). A similar federal law which says that “states may not impede access to bathing facilities” would be unforceable by private litigants, because this hypothetical law lacks the person-focused language required by Gonzaga.

If Gonzaga is still good law, then Crowther is an open and shut case. Here is the relevant language from Title IX:

No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance

Like the hypothetical law allowing dirty people to bathe, Title IX opens by stating who the law is intended to benefit (“no person in the United States”). So, under Gonzaga, Title IX may be enforced by private plaintiffs in federal court. Indeed, Gonzaga even named Title IX as an example of a law that may be enforced by private litigants because it is “phrased ‘with an unmistakable focus on the benefited class.’”

But then Medina threw the rules governing implied causes of action into bedlam. Justice Neil Gorsuch’s majority opinion in Medina neither applies the Gonzaga rule nor quotes its most important language — the line about statutes being “phrased in terms of the persons benefitted.” But it also doesn’t explicitly overrule Gonzaga.

Medina says that laws that impose obligations on institutions that accept federal funding, such as Title IX or Medicaid, are particularly unlikely to be enforceable by private litigants. But it never lays out a legal rule that judges can use to determine which of these sorts of laws are enforceable in court. Some of Gorsuch’s opinion indicates that a statute may be enforceable if it uses the magic word “right,” but his opinion never explains whether statutes that don’t use this particular word can nonetheless be enforced by private litigants.

One likely explanation for this confusing decision is that Medina was merely a cynical attempt to spite an abortion provider. The specific legal question in Medina was whether a federal law that allowed Medicaid patients to choose their health provider allowed them to choose Planned Parenthood. If the Court had applied Gonzaga in this case, Planned Parenthood would have won. So the Court’s Republicans most likely joined Gorsuch’s incomprehensible decision because they wanted to ensure that Planned Parenthood would lose.

Yet, regardless of why the Court decided to unsettle such an important area of the law in Medina, the fact remains that Medina transformed the rules governing implied causes of action into an unfathomable mess. And that means that the fate of Title IX, and the result in Crowther, is now uncertain.

So what’s the specific dispute in Crowther about?

Crowther involves two former university employees, a basketball coach and an art teacher, both of whom claim that they lost their jobs due to sex discrimination. Bizarrely, the defendants in Crowther do not actually argue that all of Title IX cannot be enforced by private plaintiffs. Instead, they claim that university employees may not bring Title IX suits.

The Crowther defendants’ primary argument is that Title IX cannot be read to permit employee suits because a different federal law, known as Title VII, also permits victims of sex discrimination in employment to sue. The defendants’ effectively argue that the existence of one law prohibiting employers from discriminating prevents Title IX from being read to also prohibit sex discrimination against school employees.

There are some important procedural differences between Title IX and Title VII suits (among other things, Title VII plaintiffs often have just 180 days to bring a claim against their employer). But the Crowther defendants are right that, because Title VII exists, universities will not become immune to sex discrimination lawsuits brought by their employees if these defendants prevail in this case.

It’s probably too much to expect this Supreme Court to confess its own error and overrule Medina, but it certainly shouldn’t compound this error.

But there’s nothing in Gonzaga, or in Medina, for that matter, which supports the proposition that two federal laws cannot overlap.

Regardless of whether this case is controlled by Gonzaga, Medina, or some other secret third thing, there is no basis in Title IX’s text for the defendants’ argument that university employees have fewer rights than, say, university students. Again, Title IX provides that “no person in the United States” shall face sex discrimination from a school that receives federal funding. Both of the plaintiffs in Crowther are people. And they are both in the United States.

If the Supreme Court buys this argument by the Crowther defendants, that will only introduce more incoherence into the rules governing implied causes of action.

Elsewhere in their brief, the Crowther defendants make the more sweeping claim that Cannon, the 1979 decision establishing that Title IX may be enforced in by private plaintiffs, is a “byproduct” of an earlier approach to implied causes of action that the Court has since discarded. If a majority of the justices accept this argument, Title IX could become virtually meaningless — as could numerous other federal statutes.

With Crowther, the Supreme Court has an opportunity to fix the chaos that Medina introduced into a highly consequential area of the law that shapes the legal rights of literally every single American. The question of which federal laws may be enforced through private lawsuits is a technical one, but it is one of the most far-reaching questions in US law because it determines whether vast swaths of federal law actually have any practical effect whatsoever.

Yet, because of the Court’s anti-abortion tirade in Medina, this crucially important area of the law is now completely incoherent. Thanks to Gorsuch’s incompetently drafted opinion in Medina, it is now impossible for lawyers to predict which laws will be deemed enforceable and which laws will be rendered inert.

It’s probably too much to expect this Supreme Court to confess its own error and overrule Medina, but it certainly shouldn’t compound this error. The Court should decide Crowther the same way it decided Cannon and all the many cases that reaffirmed Cannon’s holding. It should hold that Title IX is enforceable, full stop.
