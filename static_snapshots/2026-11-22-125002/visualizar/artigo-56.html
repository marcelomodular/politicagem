Título extraído: Is Raphael Warnock running for president?

The 2026 midterm elections were less than three weeks ago — and the new Democratic congressional majorities chosen by voters are still more than a month from taking office — but if the 2028 Democratic presidential primary isn’t fully underway, then at the very least, potential candidates are already starting to jockey for position.

Some of that maneuvering was on display earlier this month at the fittingly named “What’s Next” political conference in Denver. The conference drew Democratic operatives, strategists, and presidential hopefuls from around the country — and Vox’s America, Actually podcast, where host Astead Herndon asked attendees for their thoughts about Democratic Party’s future.

Herndon also sat down with one possible 2028 candidate: Sen. Raphael Warnock (D-GA). His background is especially relevant after the midterms: He’s the senior pastor at the historic Ebenezer Baptist Church in Atlanta at a time when more Democratic candidates are embracing religious appeals; and he’s a student of the civil rights movement, the legacy of which could be seen in surging Black turnout across the South after the Supreme Court rolled back parts of the Voting Rights Act.

Warnock has kept the door open on a presidential bid; his colleague Sen. Jon Ossoff (D-GA) is also frequently discussed as a potential candidate.

Herndon and Warnock discussed how Democrats can win in the South following election victories by Mississippi Sen.-elect Scott Colom and others; why Warnock made the trip to Michigan to campaign for Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed; and the need for presidential leadership to resolve some of America’s toughest problems at home and abroad.

Below is an excerpt of their conversation, edited for length and clarity. There’s much more in the full show, so listen to America, Actually wherever you get your podcasts or watch it on Vox’s YouTube channel.

I feel like some of the reason the South hasn’t seen some of that investment is a presumption that white Southerners are unpersuadable.

And in your race, and I think what happened in Georgia, it wasn’t just turnout, but it was also persuasion. It was convincing people to move on from Republicans and embrace Democrats. How do you make that persuasion happen?

As a pastor, there’s something that I call the ministry of presence. Sometimes you can’t solve people’s problems because they’re complicated or complex. They may take time, or you may not be able to fix it immediately. But there’s something healing about having people show up for you.

And so when I was running in 2021, and then again had to turn around and run in 2022, I would show up in these little towns all across Georgia. The folks were surprised that I was there. And you might be thinking, “Well, of course they were surprised that a Democrat was there.” No, they were surprised that anybody running for the United States Senate was in their little town.

And as we sat down and talked about their hospital that had just closed, as we talked about the struggles that their farmers were having under the tariffs that Trump imposed in his first term, we realized that we had things in common, and we shared a faith. And I got enough of those voters to build the kind of moral, multiracial coalition to win.

Some of the playbook here, though, feels kind of intuitive. You know, show up, lean in to a kind of shared identity. Campaign everywhere.

Even when I’ve heard Southerners talk about building structures over a long period of time, part of me wonders: Why has it been such an effort to make that happen? Are we talking around racism here? Is it just because these are people who have been written off?

That’s a good point, and it’s an interesting thing. Here’s what Black Southerners and white Southerners have in common — particularly poor white Southerners in these rural districts: They’re written off. Like, that’s a shared identity. And if we’re ever to have a moment in this country that transforms it into something that Dr. King called the beloved community, we’ll somehow traverse those boundaries and recognize how much we have in common and how much our interests are connected.

I thought it was really interesting how you went to Michigan to campaign for Abdul El-Sayed, who obviously was victorious in that race. You made a clear pitch to Black voters. I thought it was an interesting message. I want you to just describe it — why was that advocacy important to you?

First of all, I wanted us to win the majority. But also, as I looked at that debate as it was unfolding, I had to show up. I’m a child of the Black church. And when we say the Black church, let me hasten because people don’t know this history.

We’ve never meant anything racially exclusive about that. Black church is just a moniker for the anti-slavery church. Because almost all of American Christianity acquiesced — not just acquiesced, aided and provided a kind of moral covering, if you will, for slavery. So the Black church was literally born fighting for freedom. It was born fighting bigotry.

“We need somebody who doesn’t need the job; who really is driven by a deep sense of moral vocation and mission, and for whom the job is a set of tools.”

And when I saw the all-out assault on Brother Abdul on the basis of his religious identity — bigotry is bigotry, and religious bigotry is bigotry. The Black church was born fighting bigotry. And so you literally have people saying that he’s unfit because of where he prays and how he prays.

And if there was anybody that I felt should be the tip of the spear rejecting that idea, it was the Black church, and so I went, among other things, to remind my colleagues there of who we are.

It wasn’t just Republicans who used some of those Islamophobic attacks. There was the infamous scene of Haley Stevens, the Democratic congresswoman who lost that primary, going to the Black church [with Republican Senate candidate Mike Rogers].

Here she is standing with somebody who is engaged in this kind of Islamophobic rhetoric, and she’s in a Black church doing this. It’s deeply insulting.

We had Abdul El-Sayed on our show, and he made the case for cutting off aid to Israel. In 2025, you and Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff voted to block a measure from Bernie Sanders that would’ve stopped $8 billion in aid for Israel.

I recently saw a stat that 74 percent of Democrats oppose additional funding for Israel, per a poll from the New York Times and Siena. Was that vote a mistake?

We’ve had several [Joint Resolution of Disapproval] votes. You chose only to mention the one that I didn’t vote for. I have voted on multiple occasions to limit military aid to Israel.

My North Star where that issue is concerned is a region where Israeli mothers and Palestinian mothers can put their children to sleep at night, know that they’re safe, and have them awaken to a world that embraces their full humanity.

I support a two-state solution. Clearly, Netanyahu does not. He is a problem. and what we need in this moment is leadership. I don’t shirk that one bit. But I will tell you this, as someone who has voted for these [joint resolutions] on a few occasions in response to the moral atrocities that we’ve seen in Gaza, I can tell you that what I’ve learned is that there is no replacement for presidential leadership, and we haven’t had that on this issue in quite some time.

Would you include President Biden in that?

We have not had the kind of leadership that I would want to see on that issue in quite some time. The people are crying out for more.

I am just old enough to remember when an American president took two old men in a room at Camp David and he wouldn’t let them go until they worked something out.

Of course, Anwar Sadat paid a very high price for that. I remember that, too. I think I was in seventh grade. We need American presidential leadership in this moment for that and for a whole range of reasons.

You’ve mentioned presidential leadership. What’s one nonnegotiable for the next Democratic presidential nominee? What’s one thing you think they have to do?

We need somebody who doesn’t need the job; who really is driven by a deep sense of moral vocation and mission, and for whom the job is a set of tools. But the mission is what is central. I think when you have somebody who’s grounded in that kind of deep commitment, then we’re onto something.

I’ve seen you kind of dodge this question about whether you’re considering a run for president in 2028. Your go-to answer was, “Well, I’m focused on the midterms.” Now that we have results from the midterms, I’m going ask you again.

Are you thinking about running for president in 2028?

I remain very committed, and deeply honored to be in this fight for the people of Georgia.

You know, who gets to represent 11 million people? Stand up, get up, fight for them every day? And I remain prayerful about what’s the best use of my gifts in this moment.

I hope that at the end of my life, I will have poured it all out and gone ’till the wheels fall off. And that’s what I’m trying to do — give it all I have.

Give me a sense of how that decision calculus works. Very few people are ever in the position to think about such a thing. How do you go about making a decision about whether that’s true for you?

I have been praying this prayer since I was a kid. I ask God to order my steps. And so you just do the work, and I think you’ll know what you need to do when the time comes.
