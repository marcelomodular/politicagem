The recent summit between U.S. President Donald Trump, Russian President Vladimir Putin, and Chinese President Xi Jinping promised to “conduct a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons.” But what that means is unclear, especially when arms control itself has become a politically fraught term across multiple spheres.

The expiration of New START, a 2011 treaty that built on past arms control efforts, in February left the United States and Russia without legally binding limits on their deployed strategic nuclear forces. Some North Korea watchers worry that arms control negotiations would mean abandoning denuclearization as a policy objective, even though interim measures could contribute to the longer-term goal. Meanwhile, China has long resisted trilateral arms control talks, citing the disparity between its arsenal and those of the United States and Russia.

The Shenzen trilateral declaration nevertheless represents a modest but potentially meaningful diplomatic opening. China’s willingness to discuss nuclear dangers is noteworthy given its resistance to negotiations on nuclear arms limitations. However, the declaration provides no timetable, no delegation, and no agreement on specific measures. It remains an open question whether the three countries can translate that commitment into practical cooperation.

Arms control does not have to begin with a treaty. Arms control and nuclear risk reduction are distinct but overlapping approaches to managing nuclear competition, reducing the dangers of miscalculation, and building confidence among adversaries. Measures such as missile launch notifications and crisis communications can be pursued independently of a formal arms control treaty.

A trilateral arms control treaty remains a distant prospect because of China’s reluctance and Moscow’s demands for changes in broader U.S. policy regarding military support for Ukraine and NATO’s role in European security, and its insistence that multilateral arms control negotiations include the nuclear forces of the United Kingdom and France. But Washington, Beijing, and Moscow do not need to wait for a treaty before taking steps to reduce nuclear risks.

Two days before the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, I suggested at the Track 1.5 Stockholm China Forum that a positive outcome of a Trump-Xi-Putin meeting would be if they reaffirmed that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought (also known as the 1985 Reagan-Gorbachev principle) and that they should begin talks on nuclear risk reduction. That was the language the leaders agreed to in Shenzhen.

However, neither the principle nor the idea of risk reduction talks is new. In 2022, the P5—the five permanent members of the United Nations Security Council (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States)—had reaffirmed the same principle. Reaffirming it again was easier than agreeing on concrete measures that reduce nuclear dangers.

At least three obstacles could undermine the Shenzhen declaration: geopolitical crises, disagreements over the sources of nuclear risk and their appropriate measures, and incompatible conditions in other areas for progress on risk reduction. Take geopolitical tensions first. Beijing could suspend talks with Washington because of a confrontation over Taiwan or tensions from U.S. arms sales or military activities. A prolonged war in Ukraine and disagreements over European security could also hinder sustained dialogue and cooperation. The trilateral format creates an additional vulnerability: a crisis between any two participants could jeopardize the dialogue even if the third party wants to continue it.

The three countries may also disagree on what constitutes nuclear risk reduction and how best to achieve it. Beijing has tended to emphasize U.S. policies and military capabilities that it considers to be key sources of nuclear risk while Washington has placed greater emphasis on operational measures like establishing crisis hotlines to prevent misunderstanding, miscalculation, and unintended escalation.

Therefore, China might favor a discussion on strategic intentions, nuclear doctrines, or mutual no-first-use commitments while Washington would likely prioritize concrete measures such as missile launch notifications, reliable crisis communications, and greater transparency. These approaches are not mutually exclusive, but they reflect different assessments of what makes nuclear conflict more likely and how to manage those risks.

Some risk reduction measures require greater transparency, which China has historically rejected. Meaningful first steps in a risk reduction dialogue would be to provide advance notice of each other’s ballistic missile launches and create a crisis communications channel. However, Washington and Beijing currently do not practice advance notifications in the way that Washington and Moscow or Beijing and Moscow do.

China’s submarine-launched ballistic missile test in July illustrates the problem. While Beijing said that it notified relevant countries in advance, the notification fell short of the 24-hour standard urged by the United States and other countries.

Washington has already proposed this year that the P5 work on ballistic missile launch notifications and establish a P5 secure communications network, but it had “not yet gained consensus.” Therefore, the Shenzhen declaration raises the question of whether the three largest nuclear powers can make progress on proposals that have encountered resistance in the broader P5 process.

As for crisis communications, the challenge is not simply establishing a direct link between Washington and Beijing. Some channels already exist, but their effectiveness depends on whether urgent messages reach the appropriate decision-makers and receive timely responses. Existing models include the Washington-Moscow Direct Communications Link, which was established in 1963 at the leadership level after the Cuban missile crisis, and the Nuclear Risk Reduction Centers, which were established in 1987 to facilitate routine notifications and messages under arms control and risk reduction arrangements.

A similar arrangement simply does not exist between the United States and China. The limitations of existing channels became apparent in February 2023, when Beijing refused to answer a call from U.S. Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin, who was using a defense phone line established in 2008, to explain the U.S. shootdown of a Chinese surveillance balloon and ease tensions. The establishment of an institutionalized channel would require agreed procedures, designated authorities, and a commitment to maintain reliable communications even during periods of heightened tension.

Progress could also stall if governments make nuclear risk reduction conditional on concessions in other disputes. Beijing could seek changes in U.S. policy toward Taiwan or missile defense, while Moscow could press Washington on broader bilateral relations or on French and British nuclear forces. Such demands could hold nuclear risk reduction hostage to broader geopolitical disagreements.

Despite these obstacles, all three countries have an interest in reducing nuclear dangers. An initial trilateral dialogue could accommodate their competing priorities without requiring the three nuclear powers to agree on the exact causes of strategic instability.

They could begin by exchanging views on strategic intentions, nuclear doctrines, and behaviors that each considers destabilizing, while simultaneously exploring practical measures including missile launch notifications and crisis communications. They could also examine how the integration of artificial intelligence into military and nuclear-related systems might introduce new risks and what safeguards could help mitigate them. The immediate goal would be to identify risks that all three governments have an interest in reducing, even if they disagree on their origins.

The implications of nuclear risk reduction and prospects for eventual arms control extend beyond the United States, China, and Russia. U.S. allies are watching how Washington manages nuclear competition with Beijing and Moscow, how it responds to North Korea’s advancing nuclear capabilities, and how it maintains the credibility of its security commitments. The Shenzhen declaration’s silence on North Korea’s nuclear program raises questions about how the three nuclear powers intend to reconcile their commitment in preventing nuclear proliferation with the security concerns of the United States’ Asian allies.

The United States will need to negotiate measures with its two nuclear competitors in ways that reduce the risk of great-power conflict without weakening regional deterrence or creating new incentives for nuclear proliferation. But that requires more than a trilateral treaty. Washington will need to consider how its allies assess the implications of any future trilateral agreement for their own security, rather than treating their concerns as secondary to great-power negotiations.
