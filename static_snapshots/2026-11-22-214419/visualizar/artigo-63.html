Título extraído: Chuck Schumer vs. Chris Van Hollen: The real issue in Senate leader fight

Sen. Chuck Schumer has led the Senate Democratic caucus for 10 years, and he’s now facing the most serious challenge to his post that he’s ever encountered.

Just as Democrats are about to take their largest majority since the beginning of Barack Obama’s presidency, Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen is challenging Schumer for the role of leading that new majority. Van Hollen has won the backing of several senators so far, as well as various outside progressive groups.

In one sense, the reasons Democrats might want to move on from Schumer are obvious. Schumer is 75 years old, and is not a particularly charismatic messenger or compelling speaker. His personal positions on issues like US support for Israel, and his longtime cultivation of Wall Street and corporate donors, put him out of step with the Democratic base. And polls regularly show that he is extremely unpopular.

At the same time, the job of Senate majority leader also isn’t really about winning a national popularity contest. It’s about leading the Senate — a challenging remit that involves a multitude of tasks, including but not limited to setting the legislative calendar, crafting legislative and big-picture policy strategy, dealing with outside interest groups, coordinating with the speaker of the House and president, and trying to hold the majority in the next election.

And there’s another underappreciated part of the job: volunteering to be the one political figure who’s most likely to be hated. Many of the leaders considered especially effective in their role — from Mitch McConnell to Nancy Pelosi — were consistently unpopular, often because the position requires taking constant slings from both outside and inside their party.

It’s this last component that’s perhaps discussed the least — and in conversations with legislative veterans from across the political spectrum, I’ve been surprised how often it’s come up as a critical part of the case for choosing either of them.

The Senate majority leader’s “heat shield” function

Chuck Schumer and Mitch McConnell speak at the Capitol in February 2024. Craig Hudson/Bloomberg via Getty Images

How would Van Hollen do the job differently? Publicly, he’s touched on a few points.

In first announcing his leadership bid last week, Van Hollen told MS NOW’s Jen Psaki that Democrats should end President Donald Trump’s “illegal and senseless” Iran war this year, by giving Trump a choice: end the war, or the government gets shut down.

He said at a conference in Denver that he opposed the Senate filibuster — the rule that requires 60 votes to advance nearly all legislation — and said he would have to “talk to the caucus” about it.

And asked by NPR’s Steve Inskeep to name a specific issue in which his approach would be different from Schumer’s, Van Hollen said he would hold a floor vote on the PRO Act, a labor rights bill.

All three positions seem intended to suggest that Van Hollen would try to lead what will be a 56-member Senate caucus in a more progressive direction.

But some experts were skeptical about how well that would work.

“A leader is like a middleman,” James Wallner, a former Republican Senate aide, told me. “They’ve got to negotiate with everybody. In the Senate we haven’t really seen leaders come from a wing of the party. They usually are going to be dispositionally moderate — and that puts them in a position to negotiate with their own colleagues.”

Indeed, current and former Senate leadership aides described the job rather differently — as less about pushing the caucus to do what you want, and more about channeling what senators already want to do, crafting a consensus among them, and serving their needs.

Justin Goodman, a former Schumer aide, told me a crucial part of the job was being a “heat shield for the members when difficult decisions need to be made, and they’re difficult decisions the caucus has support for.”

The premise here is that much, and perhaps most, of what the Democratic base wants can’t happen. Either because of Trump’s veto pen, the chamber’s rules, or simply because many Democratic senators don’t actually want it to happen — the idea is that many disappointments are inevitable, and that the leader will be the fall guy for them. (Another commonly used term? A “sin-eater.”)

Take, for instance, the PRO Act — which, in 2021 and 2022, the last time Democrats controlled Washington, Schumer did indeed refuse to bring to a floor vote. According to Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-VT), Schumer told him he wouldn’t do it unless Sanders could already line up the 60 supporters necessary to beat the filibuster.

The implication is that, if the bill was certain to fail anyway, Schumer did not want to make his vulnerable members take a vote that might be uncomfortable for them — which would expose them to either the base’s fury, or to general election attack ads.

Van Hollen’s professed desire to hold a PRO Act vote would signal a different approach — one that would make progressives happy, and force some moderates to take an uncomfortable vote.

It is not so clear that that approach would be more successful at getting progressive priorities actually signed into law. (And the last time Schumer was majority leader, he did in some instances put moderates on the spot — such as in his push to change Senate rules to pass a voting rights bill, which Sens. Joe Manchin and Kyrsten Sinema voted down.)

But Schumer’s most famous example of “sin eating” occurred in March 2025, when he helped orchestrate the passage of a government funding bill, despite progressives’ desire to threaten a shutdown in hopes of fighting back against what they viewed as the new Trump administration’s authoritarian abuses. “I believe so strongly I did the right thing,” Schumer said then.

“Nobody wants the government to shut down. But a lot of people don’t want to be the ones to vote ‘yes’ to keep it open,” Goodman told me. “So Sen. Schumer, as the leader, was often the one getting the job done and taking the heat for it.”

Schumer later endorsed a more confrontational strategy that resulted in a 43-day government shutdown that fall— but though Democrats were viewed as winning the politics of that fight, some of their members grew increasingly concerned about the pain caused by lapses in government benefits.

Eventually, some Democrats peeled off and voted with the GOP to reopen the government — and though Schumer officially opposed reopening this, he still got blamed for not doing enough to stop it.

Has Schumer lost too much credibility to sell these compromises to the base?

Schumer speaks to reporters outside the US Capitol in Washington, DC, on November 18, 2026. Brendan Smialowski/AFP via Getty Images

That said, just because Schumer took on the “sin eater” role in the past doesn’t mean he’s the only one who could do it in the future. It’s possible someone more in touch with the party’s base might be better at finding consensus.

Van Hollen is backed by several of the most progressive senators and senators-elect — including Sanders, Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan, and Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota — but he and his supporters have also pushed back against attempts to frame this contest as purely one of progressives versus moderates.

Indeed, when I talked to Ezra Levin, co-founder of the progressive group Indivisible, he made a rather different case. Levin argued that, actually, Van Hollen would do a better job at selling the compromises that inevitably will have to be made to the base — including to progressives like him.

Levin said he entirely agrees that part of the next leader’s job will be to get the caucus to “do some things that are going to be greatly unpopular” — including funding the government, and raising the debt ceiling. “Senate Majority Leader Chris Van Hollen, if he does his job right, is going to piss off me and other folks a lot,” Levin said.

But, he continued, because of Schumer’s history and the longstanding lack of trust in him from the base, Schumer will simply have a harder time selling those compromises.

“If the Senate majority leader is a sponge for anger, you’ve got to change that sponge more than once a decade,” Levin said. “Or that sponge will stop being useful.”

Indeed, House Republicans have twice experienced a dynamic like this, where the base’s deep distrust of Speakers John Boehner and Kevin McCarthy resulted in Boehner’s resignation and McCarthy’s ouster. Then, their more conservative successors, Paul Ryan and Mike Johnson, advanced similar legislative compromises without facing serious revolts of their own. Getting a new face really can buy some goodwill and tamp down disquiet, for a time.

“If I’m a moderate in the Senate, I want somebody else to take the blame if we do have to push through something,” Levin said. “Who can absorb that blame? Who has credibility with the outside actors? With the grassroots groups blowing up my phone? With all the constituent parts of the Democratic Party?”
