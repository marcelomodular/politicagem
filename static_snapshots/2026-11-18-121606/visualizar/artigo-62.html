Título extraído: Why everything from Outlook to banks to airlines is going down right now.

Have you tried to take a flight in the last week? What about check your email, your bank balance, your Apple account? (Or, for a select few of us, your top-secret internal spy agency network?)

If you have, you might have noticed that some or all of those services didn’t work as expected. First, Microsoft’s cloud programs, such as Outlook, went down late last week. Then, all of the planes were grounded on Friday. And this week, we had to deal with the bank outages, a separate United Airlines-specific outage, the Apple outage, and the NSA communications outage.

As of this writing, most of these issues have now been ironed out, at least for the time being, and there’s no indication that any of them were cyberattacks or otherwise malicious. But whatever the reasons, it sure feels like there have been a lot more outages this week and last than in a usual seven-day stretch.

To get to the bottom of it — hopefully before something else shorts out — the Today, Explained team spoke with Lily Hay Newman, a senior writer at WIRED who covers hacking, information security, and digital privacy.

She spoke with Today, Explained co-host Sean Rameswaram about why it took Microsoft so long to get Outlook back on its feet, all of the other recent outages we’ve suffered, and whether it’s as unprecedented as it feels in the moment. Plus, she explains how AI is causing a broader “vulnepocalypse” (or “Vulnageddon”), even if it’s not specifically the culprit of all of our current outages.

Below is an excerpt of Newman’s conversation with Sean, edited for length and clarity. There’s much more in the full podcast, so listen to Today, Explained wherever you get your podcasts, including Apple Podcasts, Pandora, and Spotify.

Lily, we spent the first half of the show talking about the issues with our air traffic control systems, but last week, of course, also brought chaos to a lot of people’s inboxes when Outlook was not so good. Tell us what happened there.

Microsoft 365, so all of the productivity platforms, had a massive outage and it dragged on for a full day, which is very noticeable when it’s a set of products that are as integrated in everything, as 365 is.

That means you just can’t work, right? This was probably billions of dollars of productivity lost.

Yeah, this is the cloud version of Microsoft Office: Outlook, Word, Excel, Copilot, Teams. If that’s your productivity platform, then yeah, you can’t work. That’s everything.

Microsoft is so enmeshed in the tech ecosystem, even before all of the cloud platforms, because they’re the Windows maker. They’re in everything.

Microsoft, a few days later, on Monday, what they were saying was that they had sent out an update. It’s a Windows update, but it’s only really for their infrastructure. It’s not an update in consumer Windows. It’s not something your laptop would’ve gotten, but it has been out and proliferating through their infrastructure for about three months. But it actually had a flaw in it that wasn’t caught by their testing.

At the same time, they say cloud infrastructure is all about managing traffic and managing different regions of data centers. The issue with that three months of proliferation of that update started triggering and that’s why it was such an intractable issue.

At first, I saw it and I thought, okay, it’ll be a few hours, three hours or something, and they’ll fix it. And then, waking up Friday morning, it’s still not fixed. And it’s like, whoa, so something’s really going on.

And so it was the interplay of multiple factors that made it so hard for them to recover, they say.

Okay. Very different situations here between air traffic control and Microsoft 365. However, both kind of have a similar origin story in that they’re about patching systems and upgrading systems.

We’ve heard from Sean Duffy, Transportation secretary, that he cannot guarantee what happened on Friday won’t happen again in the near distant future.

Have we heard something more reassuring from Microsoft?

Microsoft would definitely say, “This isn’t going to happen again. We want to give you all assurances. We are so sorry. Sometimes problems arise because of cyber attacks or exploitation of vulnerabilities.”

And then it’s kind of like, ‘Well, why weren’t all those systems patched? There was a patch out.’

But a lot of times patching is slower than we would like because the ramifications of a bad patch or an unforeseen consequence of an update can be really severe, and so organizations tend to be very nervous.

But it’s never the last time. These types of outages and failures happen in all technology systems and they will continue to, but people try to learn as much as they can from each incident and make things more and more robust.

Maybe a good time to ask you about all of the other breakdowns we’ve been having in our technology and elsewhere over the last few weeks.

I mean, people may not have even realized they just lived through something called Black October. What is Black October, beyond Halloween?

In October there were a few things going on, including with the widely used web scripting language, PHP. There were these two vulnerabilities in it that researchers were really concerned that if they were exploited, they could cause remote access takeovers of all different websites and all different platforms.

And so there was this big, industry-wide effort to make sure that everybody was patching — a “We have to patch this right away, don’t wait” type of thing.

So there’s Black October and then there’s the “vulnepocalypse.” People have been talking about this for maybe about a year now, this idea—

We need a better name for that.

Look, it’s not my thing. I’m just doing air quotes here, heavy air quotes. But this idea that agentic AI and AI models are becoming more and more powerful and adept at finding bugs in software and in helping both researchers and, potentially, attackers figure out how to exploit those vulnerabilities and weaponize them.

There’s this [idea] that cybersecurity is always this cat and mouse — again, I’m doing the air quotes — “cat and mouse game,” or an “arms race” is another phrase that’s often used. And so that in itself is not new, but the idea that AI in the mix just really accelerates it for both sides, and how to manage that dynamic.

Then there was this Iranian cyber attack on supermarket fridges or Binance getting hacked for, I don’t know, I think the most money I’ve ever heard about in any kind of hacker heist ever — $6 billion?

I thought that we were meant to believe that crypto was unhackable, and here we are getting hacked for $6 billion?

I just want everyone to know you can rest easy. Things were already really bad before.

The fridge hacks, for example, the vulnerabilities that are being exploited there actually kind of go back numerous years. French officials have pointed to Iranian actors as having been behind at least some of those. This is targeting the food supply chain.

And same with the Binance situation. North Korean hackers stealing cryptocurrency, including large sums of cryptocurrency, is a story that reporters have been following for years and years and years.

And then on Monday, we had this bank outage across banks across the United States — Bank of America, Chase, Wells Fargo.

It just feels like everything’s breaking at a time where we’re on the precipice of driverless cars and AI running everything. Is it getting worse, or is it just a rough few months here?

To me, this stuff feels more cyclical. People may remember eight to 10 years ago when there were a ton of DDoS attacks taking down huge services. There was even basically an East Coast internet outage.

For me, it feels like we’re in another round of those, one of those moments where a lot of stuff starts to pile up and incident responders are really overworked and really exhausted and then other things are happening and nobody has as much elasticity or bandwidth because there’s so many things piling up at once — especially right now, in this new era of ubiquitous AI and access to more and more capable AI systems.

Again, it’s not to diminish that feeling. It’s just maybe helpful to have some context that it’s not truly, truly unprecedented right now.
