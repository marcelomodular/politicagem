On Wednesday, the Pentagon blew up about five tons of lead azide, an unstable explosive stored at an ammunition plant in Iowa, in order to dispose of it, sending a toxic plume of lead dust into the sky the day before Thanksgiving.

The detonation was briefly halted by a lower court before a federal circuit court cleared the way on Monday, and went forward despite serious public health concerns.

There was a real need for haste, according to the Army: The lead azide was sufficiently unstable that it was at risk of detonating on its own in an uncontrolled explosion, and falling temperatures in Iowa reportedly made such an explosion more likely. An uncontrolled explosion would have be more dangerous and unpredictable, and could have caused worse lead pollution.

At the same time, though, Iowa residents are angry and alarmed by a lack of clear information, poor communication by the Army, and the potential health hazards.

The potential for things to go sideways had President Donald Trump nervous enough that he tried to pass the decision to Rob Sand — not the current Iowa governor, but the incoming Democratic governor-elect — and let any consequences fall on his head.

The lead plume produced by the blast — which will take place inside reinforced concrete bunkers, along with other steps to mitigate the risk — could still spread as far as 100 miles from the site, according to the Washington Post.

The American Lung Association has urged anyone near the blast to remain inside for one to two days afterward, among other guidance, and on Tuesday, the Health and Human Services Department recommended that children and pregnant women evacuate from the path of the plume, which could stretch into neighboring Missouri.

The lead risk all around us

Emergency munitions detonations in the US are pretty rare — there are, thankfully, better ways to dispose of toxic materials these days (and questions about why the Army didn’t pursue them in this case).

But despite a great deal of progress in the US — including banning things like new lead paint and pipes and leaded gasoline in the 20th century — lead remains a serious public health threat.

Part of that is because of how dangerous it really is: Lead is not just a neurotoxin, but one with no safe levels of human exposure; even a little bit can be harmful, especially to children.

But as my colleague Dylan Scott reported in 2024, it also keeps popping up nearly everywhere we look, including in our food supply.

According to Dylan’s reporting, there are three big reasons for lead’s seeming inescapability: Active disregard, negligence, and the fact that it’s just really hard to get rid of. Lead was widely used in the US decades ago, before those bans on lead paint and leaded gasoline, and some of that lead is still around, in the soil, in old houses, and in other materials dating back to that time.

It’s also not a problem that the US can easily address alone, since lots of lead exposure is the result of goods being imported into the US.

“If we really want to solve this,” Drew McCartor, the director of a global public health nonprofit, told Dylan at the time, “it requires a whole-­of-­government commitment to work outside of our borders.”
