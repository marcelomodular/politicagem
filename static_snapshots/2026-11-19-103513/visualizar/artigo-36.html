Título extraído: What makes Americans feel financially ready to have kids? It’s complicated.

When it comes to wanting kids, the size of your paycheck seems to matter less than which way it’s moving.

That’s one of the key findings in the 2026 American Family Survey, released Thursday by Brigham Young University and the Deseret News, which has been conducted annually since 2015. This year’s survey was conducted by YouGov among 3,000 Americans, along with an additional 1,500 adults ages 18 to 40.

Among childless adults under 50, 44 percent of those whose finances improved over the past year said they definitely want children, compared with 24 percent of those whose finances got worse. Income made much less of a difference: 29 percent of those earning under $50,000 said they definitely want kids, compared with 32 percent of those earning $100,000 or more.

“Whether your finances are going up or down tells people more about the future than does your income now,” said Andrew Cherlin, a professor emeritus of sociology at Johns Hopkins University who studies family formation and wasn’t involved in the survey.

Young adults were also more likely than older Americans to say they need certain things in place before having a baby, like financial stability, an established career, or a house. Higher earners named even more conditions.

“Income just doesn’t determine all of this,” said Jeremy Pope, a political scientist at BYU and one of the survey’s authors. “But I do think people care about security, they just define it differently depending on their situation.”

Because parenthood is a long-term commitment, people “want to feel certain that the conditions they need to actually raise children for 18, 20, 25 years will be in place,” Karen Benjamin Guzzo, the director of the Carolina Population Center at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, told me.

It’s an understandable instinct. For decades, Americans have been told, by politicians, employers and the culture at large, that responsible people get their ducks in a row before having kids, or risk failing their children and going broke in the process. But it’s also an increasingly impossible standard to meet. Fewer people have stable jobs they can count on for years, and even people with W-2 employment and savings are less sure about what’s coming. And there’s no way to know for certain how having a kid will go.

Still, policymakers who care about family formation should take note. Measures that give people a more lasting sense of security, like making homeownership more affordable, making child care assistance permanent or passing federal paid family leave, can do far more to help people feel ready to start a family than the kinds of policies Americans have watched come and go, like the one-year expansion of the child tax credit or pandemic-era stimulus checks.

In the short term, it’s also a warning sign that persistent inflation, tariffs, and the Iran war, which have combined to lower Americans’ real wages this year, may have downstream social effects. And looking ahead, relieving workers’ anxiety about their place in the AI era isn’t just a labor story — the more confident they feel about their future earnings, the more likely they might be to envision having children.

Those who want kids may need to rethink what “ready” really looks like, but elected officials also need to do more to make people feel secure enough to try.

A future that’s hard to picture

The American Family Survey asked people whether their finances had gotten better, worse or stayed the same over the past year. Those whose finances had gotten worse were the least likely to say they definitely wanted kids, and those whose finances had improved were the most likely.

Pope said the finding intrigued him and that more research is needed, but he has a theory. “I do think people think prospectively about this,” he told me. “I do think small changes make a difference to what you believe about the future.”

For a lot of young adults, that future is hard to picture. About a third of childless adults under 40 who want kids told the survey researchers they couldn’t say when, or whether, they’d ever feel financially ready. And that share didn’t shrink as people got older. It stayed around 30 percent from their late 20s through their late 30s.

Other recent research supports the idea that young people feel less sure about what’s coming. The Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia found this month that young adults’ sense of financial security and their outlook on the future have fallen more sharply than older adults’ since early 2025. And this fall’s Harvard Youth Poll found that, for the first time in its history, slightly more Americans ages 18 to 29 expect to be worse off financially than their parents than expect to be better off.

The American Family Survey doesn’t prove that concerns about the future are keeping people from wanting kids. Melissa Kearney, an economist at the University of Notre Dame who studies birth rates, told me the findings “could also be showing that people who want kids are more likely to [be] on a positive financial trajectory, and people who don’t want kids are subject to more uncertainty or downside financial risk.”

Pope thinks that’s less likely but couldn’t rule it out. He also noted that the survey captures what people say they want, and people who express a general interest in having kids might not follow through. “It doesn’t mean you’re out there buying prenatal vitamins or something,” he said.

More money, longer checklists

The survey also gave people a list of eight things, such as financial stability, a career, a house, or marriage, and asked which ones they consider extremely important to have before a first child.

Young adults identified more of them than older Americans did. Financial stability was at the top, chosen by 68 percent of adults under 40. They were also much more likely than people over 40 to say they needed an established career first (48 percent versus 32 percent) or a house (30 percent versus 20 percent).

Higher earners actually had longer checklists. Young adults earning $100,000 or more picked an average of 3.9 items they considered extremely important to have in place first, compared with 3.3 for those earning under $50,000. Cherlin, who has long written about marriage becoming a “capstone” of adult life rather than an early foundation, said the same thing has happened with kids. For wealthier young adults, he told me, having a child is “something you only do when the rest of your life is in order.”

People with higher incomes don’t worry as much about providing kids the basics, Cherlin added, “but they are concerned about whether they can maintain their middle-class lifestyles.”

Kearney offered a related explanation. “It’s all relative,” she said. High earners “might simply have an even higher bar for what they need to feel economically secure or sufficiently ‘ready’ to bring children into their lives.”

Kearney’s own research adds another wrinkle. In a forthcoming paper, she and her co-authors found that never-married, childless adults in their late 20s and early 30s didn’t rate a hypothetical life with children any more favorably when it also came with ample finances or a home, but they did when it came with a fulfilling relationship.

To her, it’s another sign that money isn’t the whole story. “While income and prices still matter, something beyond income and prices is driving the generational shift that we’re witnessing in the family formation decisions of young adults,” she said.

Money still matters, though, and many Americans are struggling. This year, 42 percent told the American Family Survey they’d faced a serious financial hardship in the past year, like going hungry, missing an important bill, or skipping medical care because of cost. That’s up from 35 percent last year, and the highest since the survey started asking in 2016.

Lower-income young adults had it worst. Among those ages 18 to 40 earning under $50,000, nearly two-thirds reported a hardship, about double the rate for those earning $100,000 or more.

But Pope told me not to read too deeply into that finding. He and his co-author changed how they asked about hardships this year, and he thinks that may have made the share go up. Pope is “not prepared to lean terribly hard on that data” until there’s another year to compare. Still, he believes hardship has been rising since the pandemic.

Guzzo said financial pressure comes up constantly in her research with childless young adults, pointing to “the general cost of living, the cost of housing, the cost of childcare, the level of debt people have, [and] having a stable job that pays decently.”

Childcare, in particular, is already shaping family decisions. Half of parents with a child under 12 told the American Family Survey they’d made a sacrifice because of childcare costs, such as cutting back on work, quitting, or turning down a promotion. Fifteen percent said they’d delayed having another child.

Pope said childcare is often what’s most pressing for parents of young kids. He also thinks people have learned not to count on government help after watching pandemic aid disappear.

“Sometimes the government gives money and sometimes it doesn’t, and people see that we can’t necessarily count on that,” he said. A permanent childcare program, he said, is something “people would think they can rely upon.”

Having a child, Pope added, is “in a sense a forever commitment.” People weigh big decisions differently depending on how long they’ll last, he said, “and policymakers should think about family supports in the same way.”
