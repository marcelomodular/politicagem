Título extraído: Despite the Nvidia Spy Scandal, America Can’t Racially Profile Its Way to AI Supremacy

A colleague in the intelligence community once jokingly called me a “Chinese spy.” His casual remark is burned into my memory. I am a Taiwanese American who has served as a U.S. intelligence officer and worked on semiconductor policy for the House Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party. Even while serving this country, my loyalty could become a punchline.

The Jensen Huang scandal now tests Washington’s ability to keep that reflex out of national security policy. He resigned as Nvidia’s CEO on Nov. 12, just days after reports of his relationships with businesswoman Alice Huang and a company executive. The Jensen Huang scandal now tests Washington’s ability to keep that reflex out of national security policy. He resigned as Nvidia’s CEO on Nov. 12, just days after reports of his relationships with businesswoman Alice Huang and a company executive. Yet his abrupt departure leaves serious questions related to Chinese espionage unresolved. Investigators must still determine whether Jensen Huang helped Beijing obtain secrets or influence.

In the meantime, public discourse in the United States is conflating the potential for Chinese coercion with the proof of its existence, even going so far as to promote ethnic scapegoating. The growing hysteria is increasingly focused on the threat posed by anyone at all associated with China. The irony is that this will eventually, in concrete ways, make Americans less secure.

News reports  described intercepted communications suggesting that Chinese intelligence received information from someone close to Jensen Huang and Nvidia. It also reported that federal officials have been investigating whether Alice Huang’s Megaspeed has been potentially diverting restricted Nvidia chips to China since 2024. If true, Washington should prosecute those involved and close relevant loopholes.

Reporting has also described, however, a troubling divide within the U.S. government. Some intelligence officials considered the spy label “over-torqued.” Attorney General Todd Blanche and Rep. Jim Himes, a ranking member of the House Intelligence Committee, also weighed in on the day that Jensen Huang resigned, saying neither the U.S. Justice Department nor the intelligence community had concluded she was a spy; while her reported proximity to Jensen Huang justifies scrutiny, it cannot by itself prove that she worked for Beijing.

Either way, nothing about this case makes all people of Asian descent suspect. Jensen Huang’s conduct deserves scrutiny; the loyalty of millions who share his ancestry does not. And yet Congress is beginning to overstep from proper oversight into broader exclusion. Rep. Riley Moore’s bill, for example, proposes denying student and exchange visas for research or study to Chinese nationals. Adopting that approach in response to Nvidia solely because of someone’s nationality fails to address actual security vulnerabilities.

Washington could and should proactively mitigate threats; foreign intelligence ties, risky financial exposure, and transferring unauthorized information are legitimate reasons to restrict sensitive access. But the idea that every Chinese citizen could be a spy dangerously conflates vulnerability with culpability.

It’s true that China’s national intelligence law compels citizens and organizations to assist intelligence work. Beijing can pressure its citizens, but that does not automatically make them a Chinese agent. In 2018, William Evanina, then the United States’ top counterintelligence official, noted that 99.9 percent of Chinese students in the United States were “here legitimately and doing great research.” Most may never be approached by China’s Ministry of State Security, and others might simply refuse or report such encounters. Effective counterintelligence cannot consider these all as equivalent.

The current public discourse is also actively blurring the clear distinction between Chinese ancestry and Chinese citizenship. The Asian American Journalists Association called out headlines that turned Jensen Huang’s surname into an espionage pun. On X, a viral collage highlighted prominent executives married to women of Chinese descent with the caption, “I have one question… actually, a LOT of questions.” Another post invoked “Chinese honeypot espionage” in reference to OpenAI CEO Sam Altman’s former roommate, Chinese American AI executive Alexandr Wang.

In a 2019 bill that Trump signed into law, Congress warned that profiling plays right into Beijing’s narrative that the global Chinese diaspora owes it loyalty. Washington should not validate that claim.

Blanket suspicion makes legitimate threats harder to identify. MITRE’s insider threat researchers warned that indiscriminate threat indicators overwhelm security with false positives. Nationality alone cannot distinguish a Chinese agent from a dissident resisting Beijing. Pursuing imagined spies can leave real ones undetected.

Profiling also silences those best positioned to expose espionage. The FBI warns of various ways that foreign governments may coerce their citizens in the United States to serve their interests. A threatened scientist could help investigators neutralize foreign intelligence activity early. If speaking up invites suspicion, though, they will likely remain silent.

Trust is critical to improving security. In April 2025, Taiwan’s Defense Ministry found that voluntary reporting prompted 87.5 percent of national security investigations. Beijing’s preferred targets are often best positioned to expose its operations. Washington should make reporting foreign coercion safer through confidential channels backed by legal and security assistance.

Unwarranted suspicion also threatens the United States’ AI talent lead over China. A recent  study by Carnegie Endowment found that 57 percent of authors accepted to NeurIPS 2025, one of the most elite AI conferences, had earned undergraduate degrees in China. Only 13 percent had gone to college in the United States. Furthermore, China now hosts 41 percent of the world’s top AI researchers. The United States is down to 34 percent, flipping its 2022 advantage. Washington cannot afford to alienate these scientists.

A 2023 study found that 89 percent of Chinese researchers at U.S. universities desired American scientific leadership, yet nearly two-thirds had considered leaving the country. The United States risks losing the scientists who want to help it lead. Treating them as presumptive threats could hand the U.S. talent advantage to Beijing.

The United States has made this mistake before. In 1950, Washington stripped Qian Xuesen, one of the country’s leading rocket scientists, of his security clearance. The FBI suspected that he was tied to communists but failed to uncover evidence of espionage. Due to his rocketry expertise, however, security officials struggled between deporting or detaining him. When Qian finally left in 1955, he spearheaded China’s formidable missile and space programs. Washington considered him too dangerous to trust and too valuable to let go. It lost him anyway.

Before pursuing charges or major research sanctions, security officers should mandate independent technical review and offer researchers due process, presuming innocence until proven guilty. In 2015, prosecutors charged Temple University physicist Xiaoxing Xi with sending proprietary technology to China. They eventually dropped the case after finding that he actually shared nonsensitive devices, but his reputation was already destroyed.

Congress already requires that federal research agencies avoid discriminating based on race, ethnicity, or national origin. Yet a January 2026 Government Accountability Office report found that five major agencies had still not evaluated their anti-discrimination safeguards. Washington should conduct those assessments to ensure all researchers are equally scrutinized based on their conduct.

Jensen Huang’s successes and failures do not make Asian Americans more or less American. Washington can and should investigate espionage without ethnically profiling Asian Americans. Otherwise, the United States risks overlooking real spies, silencing whistleblowers, and driving away world-class talent. The country’s great strength is its ability to distinguish friend from foe. It cannot afford to confuse the two.
