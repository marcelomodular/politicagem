Título extraído: Risk and Reward Along Africa’s Red Sea Littoral

The new era of maritime chokepoint disruption has, in many ways, highlighted the importance of Africa’s Red Sea and Gulf of Aden states. The Houthis’ ability to hold the Bab el-Mandeb at risk, combined with the deepening rivalry between the Abraham and Mecca blocs of the Middle East, have created powerful incentives for a range of extra-regional actors to further expand their partnerships along the Horn of Africa’s coastline. This is particularly clear in the southern Red Sea and Gulf of Aden, where the United Arab Emirates, Israel, and the United States have grown their diplomatic and military engagement in Puntland and Somaliland, and where the United States has pursued a diplomatic rapprochement with Eritrea.

What does this mean for the Horn of Africa’s littoral states? Although their geographic proximity to what Bruno Macaes called “the strait at the center of the world” has emerged as valuable strategic currency, it is not hard to see how the attempt to coopt Africa’s Red Sea states into one alliance or another could go wrong. The concerns are threefold: that the Horn’s littoral states might become entrapped in conflicts that are not their own; that the intra-Horn rivalries the littoral states are already enmeshed in are made more acute; and that internal governance conditions in the Horn’s states further deteriorate.

The Horn’s littoral states cannot wish away geography or the incentives that it creates. But they do have room to maneuver. Robust public debate among Horn publics, prudent decision-making from ruling elites, and conscientious engagement from key external partners can ensure that this new chapter of geopolitical centrality does not further destabilize a region that is already at the precipice.

The Horn of Africa has been a critical node since the inauguration of the Suez Canal in 1869. But the intensity with which this fact has been felt has waxed and waned over time. The start of the Yemen War in 2015 initiated another period of growing entanglement between the Horn of Africa and a range of outside powers, each of whom sought a strategic perch near the Red Sea corridor from which to project their security and commercial interests.

But the turmoil in the  Middle East after Oct. 7, 2023, has sharpened this dynamic. Two developments have been particularly consequential. The first is the Houthi campaign to interrupt maritime traffic in the southern Red Sea and Gulf of Aden, with a first wave of attacks beginning in November 2023 and a second wave targeting Saudi shipping starting in the summer of 2026. The second dynamic has been the deepening rivalry between the Abraham and Mecca blocs of the Middle East. Although these respective blocs are not entirely coherent, there are clear divisions over Israel’s increasingly muscular security policy; the Iran crisis; and their respective interventions in conflicts between national governments and subnational armed actors across the region.

In the face of new strategic pressures, several extra-regional actors have sought enhanced access and position in this zone, over and above what they had secured in preceding years. A triumvirate of littoral countries/de-facto states have been immediate targets: Somaliland, Puntland, and Eritrea.

The impact is most apparent in Somaliland. Its economic partnership with the UAE, through the latter’s investment in the Berbera corridor, is long-standing. But this relationship has become increasingly securitized since 2024. The clearest evidence is what transpired at Berbera Airport, which has come under the effective security control of the UAE. Satellite imagery suggests the construction of an adjoining dock and the hardening of the airport’s infrastructure, including the development of underground bunkers that are potentially for ammunition and fuel. Many believe the airport has also become a logistical node in the UAE’s broader military supply network across East Africa, enabling the delivery of weapons to Sudan and Ethiopia.

Israel’s expanding role in Somaliland has run in parallel with the growing Emirati military presence. Israel diplomatically recognized Somaliland in December 2025—making it the first country in the world to do so—and some reports suggest an active Israeli intelligence presence in Berbera and the use of the airport for refueling its jets during the Iran war. Somaliland officials generally do not confirm these reports but acknowledge that an Israeli military presence is an eventual possibility.

Washington has also joined the club. In the last year, Somaliland has hosted the commanding generals of AFRICOM and SOCAFRICA. A number of U.S. military personnel based at the Baledogle Airfield in Somalia have now been redeployed to Somaliland, prompting one senior Somaliland official to privately quip, “Team Mogadishu has now become Team Hargeisa.” U.S. training opportunities for Somaliland’s security forces appear to have also grown. These developments are still nascent and as such do not yet involve the deployment of critical intelligence, security, and reconnaissance capabilities, but the direction of travel is fairly clear.

The Emirati government’s formula in Puntland has largely tracked its approach to Somaliland. Beginning in the 2010s, the Emirati footprint in the statelet had mostly consisted of a commercial port concession in Bosaso and training Puntland’s Maritime Security Forces. But from 2024 onward, the UAE scaled up its activity in Bosaso, turning an airbase next to the civilian airport into another logistical hub through which weapons, ammunition, and personnel flowed to Emirati allies throughout the Horn. The UAE has also supported Puntland’s 2024-2025 offensive against Islamic State contingents in the region through direct drone strikes. The United States has also directly backed counter-Islamic State military operations, and according to Puntland authorities, agreed in August 2026 to establish a military facility at the same Bosaso airbase that houses an Emirati presence.

Eritrea’s incorporation into this picture looks a bit different. Its external alignments sharply diverge from Somaliland and Puntland, and there is currently no evidence that Eritrea is poised to play host to foreign military facilities. Yet if the past is prologue, then Eritrea could soon find itself housing a more significant military presence. There are unconfirmed reports of the potential for an increased Egyptian presence in the southern port of Assab. Assab served as the logistical fulcrum of Emirati military operations in Yemen, and back in the early 2000s, the Eritrean government lobbied the United States to establish a military base in Eritrea. At the time, Washington declined the Eritrean offer and established its premiere African military base in neighboring Djibouti.

Maritime exigencies in and around the Bab el-Mandeb have now pushed Eritrea higher on the Trump administration’s agenda. While U.S.-Eritrea relations have been relatively poor for more than two decades, Washington allowed a Tigray-related sanctions package targeting the Eritrean government to expire in September 2026. This was a clear bid to enhance relations with Asmara and was tied to a broad, if not clearly defined, sense that Eritrea mattered for Red Sea security. This sanctions removal occurred even though some important U.S. policymakers believed that Eritrea had not really done enough to warrant such action.

Somaliland, Puntland, and Eritrea will probably extract clear strategic rewards from their emerging geopolitical centrality and the related expansion of security partnerships. All three face increasingly acute security dilemmas from what they regard as revanchist neighbors and their respective external backers. All of their national political projects are also at critical junctures: Somaliland is attempting to build momentum for wider recognition; Puntland is maneuvering to protect its autonomy within a Somali federation; and Eritrea is working to fully break its political estrangement from much of the international system. Somaliland and Puntland have even leveraged the good offices of Abu Dhabi and Washington to improve a bilateral relationship long riddled with tension.

The risks, however, are very real. The first is that these small polities quickly find themselves entrapped in conflicts that are not their own. The concern is most relevant to Somaliland, where the intensification of the Emirati, Israeli, and U.S. security presence is clearest. Though Houthi attacks in the southern Red Sea and Gulf of Aden have had negative economic repercussions on Berbera port, Somaliland’s authorities have no interest in getting involved in a regional war against the Houthis. But they might not have a choice. The Houthis themselves have threatened to strike any Israeli military presence in Somaliland and even hacked the website of Somaliland’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs in early July. If a conflict led to missile or drone strikes on Berbera, it would have a catastrophic impact on trade, government revenue, and oil imports.

There is also a risk that Somaliland’s deepening security engagement with the Abraham bloc alienates it from Saudi Arabia, which is clearly unnerved by the growing security presence of the Emiratis and Israelis in the southern Red Sea. Somaliland’s critical livestock export sector, which provides 80 percent of all export earnings, is oriented toward the Saudi market, meaning it can ill afford to antagonize Riyadh.

Somaliland’s authorities are no doubt hoping to manage the scale and conduct of the foreign military element within its borders, so as to not provoke retaliation from actors they otherwise have few differences with. Although not widely known, when it was negotiating a memorandum of understanding for sea access with Ethiopia in late 2023, Somaliland placed real limitations on an Ethiopian naval presence in its Gulf of Aden shores. This included the denial of a land corridor between the Ethiopian border and naval base, as well as limits on the Ethiopian military’s interactions with local communities and third-party military activity. But does Somaliland have the autonomy to fully constrain what Israel, the UAE, and the United States do within its borders?

The second risk is that external actors’ push for new Red Sea-oriented partnerships might inadvertently fuel escalatory dynamics in the region. This is most evident in the growing tensions between Eritrea and Ethiopia. The scramble for positions along the Horn’s Red Sea littoral has provided landlocked Ethiopia a new set of justifications to press its maritime claims, including to the port of Assab in Eritrea. Ethiopian officials have argued that since the Red Sea and its Horn ports are increasingly contested terrain, an Ethiopian naval presence is the only way to ensure the country’s economic and security interests. They also make the point that if it is legitimate for outside powers to establish military bases along the Red Sea littoral, then it is an equally legitimate course of action for Ethiopia.

At the same time, U.S. sanctions removal, and the growing proximity between Cairo and Asmara, has increased Eritrea’s willingness to risk conflict with its southern neighbor. Indeed, the decision by the TPLF and the Alliance for Survival to launch offensives on Sept. 23, likely with the approval of Asmara, came just six days after the U.S. sanctions regime on Eritrea expired.

Meanwhile, Emirati-Israeli involvement in Somaliland and Puntland has further consolidated Mogadishu’s increasing all-weather alliance with Turkey. President Hassan Sheikh has used Somalia’s growing defense partnerships with Turkey, which has deployed F-16s to the region to fight Al-Shabaab, to put pressure on Somaliland and Puntland. Most recently, this has involved an electronic visa scheme managed by Mogadishu to control the movement of foreign passport holders into Somaliland, and a reported agreement with Ankara to build a military base in Laasqoray, which sits in territory that Somaliland considers its own.

As a result, the risk of conflict between Mogadishu, Garowe, and even Hargeisa has increased considerably. In July and August, for instance, there were military confrontations between Puntland’s government and the Puntland Security Force, a militia that had increasingly come under the sway of Mogadishu, resulting in the PSF bases in Galkayo and Bosaso being seized by Puntland authorities.

A final risk is related to the core governance questions that will shape the regional security landscape of the Horn in the coming years.

Consider Eritrea, which has been mired in a deep human rights crisis for decades. This has driven hundreds of thousands of its young people abroad and produced deep, if still suppressed, social fissures. The country has no functioning constitution or electoral system, and thus no mechanism for peacefully navigating succession or adjudicating domestic political differences. Its political elite, in power since 1991, is unwilling to cultivate younger leaders or embrace basic economic and political reforms, offering little more than time-worn cliches about the need to defend against sabotage by external enemies.  Unsurprisingly, U.S. sanctions relief engages none of these issues.

In Somaliland and Puntland, the problem is one of political polarization, as deepening external partnerships have widened disagreements between contending political factions. Somaliland’s recognition deal with Israel has drawn internal criticism, including from religious actors, on both moral and strategic grounds. This was particularly true after Hargeisa made the controversial decision to place its embassy in Jerusalem rather than Tel Aviv in July. In Puntland, some opponents of President Saeed Deni believe he is using Emirati patronage to further consolidate his power. In all of these debates and more, fairly explosive clan rivalries lurk just beneath the surface.

The Horn’s littoral states can navigate the risks associated with their emerging geopolitical centrality. But it will require more than business as usual. The first step is robust public debate about the risks and rewards of external security entanglements, so that all relevant considerations are surfaced. In many ways, Somaliland is ahead of the curve in this regard, a reflection of its relatively strong—although far from perfect—democratic system.

Ruling elites must also prudently assess the costs and benefits of foreign basing and military cooperation. The challenge here is that the democratic deficit in some of these states means that regime security often prevails over public interest. There are also separate questions of state capacity in some of these polities and the fundamental ability of the government to recognize substantive problems and devise responses. Finally, external partners, particularly the United States, must recognize that the transactionalism of the moment will not deliver stability in one of the world’s most essential maritime corridors. The only solution is to encourage better and more accountable governance along Africa’s Red Sea and Gulf of Aden littoral.
