Video games are cultural juggernauts, and governments have noticed. As industry consultant George Osborn points out in his new book, Power Play: Video Games, Politics, and the Battle for Global Influence, video gaming, once a solitary activity, has become a largely communal one. Online play creates a “third place” where people, especially the young, can mingle. As Osborn puts it, “what happens in video games communities bleeds out into reality—all the way into our political discourse.”

Under Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, for instance, Saudi Arabia has become a major force in video games, mostly through the power of capital. The prince, himself a keen gamer, is trying to encourage the creation of a domestic industry. He’s had little success so far, but Saudi money has succeeded in buying up existing companies, such as the giant Electronic Arts for $55 billion and the company that created Pokémon Go for $3.5 billion.

Mohammed bin Salman wants to make money. But, as Osborn points out, he also wants to get gullible or greedy Westerners to help whitewash his corrupt, misogynistic autocracy. Buying gaming companies may be a more expensive route than donating to D.C. think tanks, but also perhaps a more effective one. Some industry insiders have already flagged worries about Saudi censorship in their games.

But if the Saudi government is shaping video games, the influence is going the other way as well. Consider some of the crown prince’s grandiose projects. His plan for The Line, a 106-mile-long and 650-foot-wide city, appeared to be inspired by the futuristic cities that appear in games like Cyberpunk 2077 and Deus Ex. The difference, of course, was that in reality The Line project was suspended after only 1.5 miles of foundation were dug.

That feedback loop between gaming and reality is also evident when looking at another of Osborn’s case studies, Russia. Especially since the invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Russian studios have churned out propaganda games such as Squad 22: ZOV, which Osborn describes as an “objectively bad video game” but also a hub for anti-Ukrainian memes and a gathering place for ultranationalists.

The fantasies of Russian games hook back into wider influence efforts. With the help of influencers such as streamer GrishaPutin, these games aim to boost morale and recruitment while also luring in foreign fighters, roughly 11,000 of whom have died fighting on the Russian side. Australian Daryl Fladung has been missing, believed dead, for several months after being recruited to come and fight for Russia (and post propaganda videos) through online war games. His last videos show him terrified and in pain.

Russia’s reactionary politics are also attractive to some gamers. The gender split in gaming is roughly equal nowadays but the third place of popular games has a habit of becoming dominated by the loudest young men. It’s not uncommon for female players to conceal their gender, or even use a voice changer, to avoid harassment.

That too has bled back into politics. Video game writers are generally liberal progressives, and there was a big push toward broadening representation in games in the 2010s, with female protagonists and people of color becoming more normalized. This was welcomed by many gamers but prompted a reactionary fury from others. The movement that resulted was Gamergate, which became part of the far-right’s attempt to woo younger male voters, with the encouragement of Steve Bannon. While it may have helped tip elections toward Donald Trump, it failed to produce any good games of its own.

Despite being the biggest video game market in the world, compared to Moscow, Beijing is far more reluctant to throw its weight around in gaming propaganda overseas. That’s in part because of a puritan communist dislike of any form of fun, leading to repeated crackdowns on minors playing games online and long delays before new games are officially approved.

Censorship at home is massive but, as Osborn notes, “Chinese games businesses invested billions of dollars in games businesses across the world to soften the blows landing on them from the CCP.” (pg. 151). Apart from some policing around Hong Kong and Taiwanese representation in games, those investments have mostly remained business-focused rather than trying to impose political limits. As with much else, China’s focus has been domestic control first, and foreign influence second.

But perhaps gaming’s biggest global influence lies in its role driving the AI industry, a story Osborn doesn’t cover in depth because much of it is so recent. Gaming depends on video cards, which are built with the high-end chips that also fuel the AI revolution. In fiscal year 2018, for instance, Nvidia made roughly 57 percent of its income from gaming. Last year, that figure was 7 percent, with roughly 90 percent coming from AI data centers.

Osborn highlights the role gaming played in driving forward the innovation needed for newer and better technology, especially in chip design. But the shift of the industry toward AI has left gamers deeply angry at Nvidia, a company they once adored. (As a result, some of the best investigative reporting on Jensen Huang’s fall and Nvidia’s role in evading U.S. sanctions on China has come out of the games media.) Gaming hardware used to be getting cheaper and cheaper; the same setup would be half the price two years later. Thanks to the AI race, the reverse is now true. The gaming PC I bought for $1,600 the week Trump was elected for a second time, anticipating tariff rises, now retails at $2,600. It might seem a little silly to care deeply about your toys getting more expensive. But the contestation between gamers and AI companies is a microcosm of a wider issue: Who controls technological power?

Personal computers have been the domain of tinkerers and inventors since they were first introduced at home. Now Sam Altman has put forward, then rapidly retracted, the idea that having RAM sitting in your own PC is “terrible” because it should be used by mega-corporations instead. In this context, the growing revolt against AI in video games—with players boycotting games that use AI art, AI writing, or even AI code—feels like part of something bigger.

Osborn’s final chapters are dedicated to promoting games that educate citizens and support the fight for democracy worldwide, from pro-Ukrainian stories to “green game jams” to promote climate awareness. But as long as the industry, and the technology that powers it, is in the hands of autocrats and tech tycoons, these aren’t the games we’ll be playing.
