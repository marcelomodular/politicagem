Título extraído: Van Hollen challenges Schumer for majority leader: The progressive case for new Democratic leadership, explained

After historic wins in the midterm elections, Democrats now have to decide who will lead their new majorities in the House and the Senate. The House race is expected to be a formality, with current minority leader Hakeem Jeffries as the sole declared candidate for House speaker.

The Senate race, however, is shaping up to be more interesting: In the upper chamber, current minority leader Chuck Schumer is facing a challenge from Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen, who argues that Senate Democrats need new leadership to meet the demands of voters who delivered them a majority.

“The call was really to reject the status quo and to reject business as usual,” Van Hollen said. “And to do that, I think we need new leadership.”

Van Hollen is also the favored candidate of the party’s progressive faction, many members of which have soured on Schumer. Corbin Trent, a former communications director for Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and the founder of the super PAC A Fight Worth Having, is one of those: He tells Today, Explained that Schumer’s and Jeffries’s style of leadership is ill-suited for this moment, and he wants to see new Democratic leaders in both chambers. He also argues that Democrats should embrace a more proactive style of governing that harkens back to the New Deal era.

Trent spoke with Today, Explained co-host Sean Rameswaram about who he wants to see lead the Democrats instead, why consensus-building is not enough, and why now is the right time to have this fight.

Below is an excerpt of their conversation, edited for length and clarity. There’s much more in the full podcast, so listen to Today, Explained wherever you get your podcasts, including Apple Podcasts, Pandora, and Spotify.

Who do you think should be leading Democrats in the House and Senate in the new year?

I think that this moment calls for some sort of leadership that shows that we have a plan, an agenda, and a vision that’s capable of taking on and meeting this moment. And it’s not Hakeem Jeffries. It’s not Chuck Schumer.

I know that’s the way we do things in the House, that’s the way we do things in the Senate. But look at the popularity of both of those institutions and then think to yourself, maybe we should do things a little differently.

Why not Hakeem Jeffries and Chuck Schumer?

Hakeem Jeffries has proven either one of two things. Either he’s incapable of wrangling the caucus, or his priorities are very different than the Democratic Party and the independent voters that voted for the Democrats in this election.

What I mean by that is we’ve had three shots at stripping war powers away from Donald Trump vis-a-vis Iran. And, oh, lo and behold, one, two, three Democrats defected

He is not supporting an arms embargo to Israel. He’s not a guy who is representing the will of the people writ large. He is very much the establishment Democrat that I think led to Donald Trump being elected twice.

Chuck Schumer is a man with a strongly worded letter in his back pocket. He’s the man that wants to pick up a couple of voters in rural Pennsylvania that are Republicans. Neither of them have a vision for building a Democratic supermajority that is akin to the one that was built around the New Deal.

I think that this moment is very similar to that moment in that it is rife with opportunity. People are hopeful, people are ready for change, but the problem with hope is that when it’s dashed, it calcifies and it becomes resentment and anger.

And Democrats seem to miss that every time.

Over and over and over and over. I can remember how inspired I was by Barack Obama during his first presidential campaign — so inspired that I got involved in politics. I went to an event in Asheville to watch him speak. I started raising money for him on his portal, and I had a watch party.

It was not my thing, but he inspired me to do all this stuff. And then several months later, what I’m hearing is a very different thing as a president than as a candidate. So we can’t keep missing these opportunities, in my mind.

Why do Jeffries and Schumer seem so confident in this moment? Schumer has a challenger, Chris Van Hollen. Hakeem kind of has one in Ayanna Pressley, but they don’t seem very worried that their positions might be in jeopardy right now. How come?

Because that’s just not the way things are done in the House, and they I think have faith that things will continue being done the way they are. Momentum, norms, all of these things point to them not having any real problem getting their leadership taken away.

But Joe Crowley also felt that way. And then famously AOC spanked his little bottom in 2018, and now he’s a consultant instead of a congressman. Now we’d be having a very different conversation, because he was the one that was in line to be the next speaker of the House.

AOC is backing Jeffries. How come? What’s your reaction to that?

I mean, is she backing Jeffries? She’s doing this weird little thread-the-needle: “He’s the only one. What can we do?”

But that’s the exact kind of thinking that you’re saying Democrats should rebuff.

Absolutely, they should. And interestingly, the most important thing to learn from Nancy Pelosi in my mind is that power is not given, it is taken. Power is not something that people give you. It is something that you take and then utilize.

The fact is that [the Congressional Progressive Caucus] has 99 members, and it is going to have more. The fact is that these members have the capacity to generate more media than most.

They can either take this moment to build power for the American people that ends up building a super-majoritarian Democratic Party, one that can make the Republican Party small enough to drown in a bathtub, or they can try to do business as usual, work with Hakeem, work with Schumer, try to extract some concessions, which they will not give.

Because like you say, they have no fear. They do not fear these people. They respond to threats and fear, fear of losing their job.

Help us understand why someone like AOC doesn’t want this job. I mean, she’s one of the most visible Democrats in the country, and surely she’d be a popular candidate, right?

I can’t explain why people do what they do. Even I can’t explain why I do what I do, let alone other people.

It doesn’t sound like a fun job. I can understand not wanting it. There is a lot of potential downside. You’re going to get ire because you’ve got to either deliver, which is going to be very difficult with Donald Trump still in the White House, or you’ve got to be really good at working to show that you’re attempting to deliver something big and you’re being blocked and by whom.

The problem is a lot of people that are going to be standing in your way are going to be Democratic representatives and Democratic senators. Because the thing that I have come to understand is that there is a team of people that want to make this country fundamentally different, fundamentally more fair: Look at the healthcare system that we have and fundamentally rethink it. Look at the economy we have with AI and robotics burgeoning and fundamentally rethink it.

But that team right now isn’t very big and it’s not like, oh, all Democrats are on board with this vision. They’re not.

You point at something that I think is relevant here, which is that this job of speaker of the House, Senate majority leader, it isn’t a walk in the park. It takes a certain type, perhaps. Maybe it takes a consensus builder.

We had Rahm Emanuel on the show after the midterms and he said that Hakeem Jeffries was that. Do you think it takes someone in the middle of all of this to suss out where the caucus is and that’s why maybe we’re not getting the most exciting people, but we’re getting people who’ve been around for a while?

Well, according to [incoming Rep.] Don Leonard, there’s a different tactic that this particular leadership uses to bring consensus. And it’s not the hand-holding, middle-of-the-road consensus-building. It’s threats: threats of making your life a living hell for two years, threats of not getting any committee assignments, threats of not getting gavels.

Ultimately, this gave us Romneycare. This kind of middle-of-the-road nonsense gave us something that got rebranded as Obamacare, and it has not slowed down the increase of our costs. It saved lives.

And here’s the problem with that: You had a Democratic caucus with 59 or 60 votes in the Senate and a majority in the House. And you had Barack Obama in there on a wave election of change. People like me got in there. And what the reasonable people were able to tell you as a new member or as a member that wanted, say, a public option or something else is that 50,000 to 60,000 people a year die from lack of healthcare. This can save 4,000 to 5,000 of those lives. Are you willing to let 4,000 or 5,000 people a year die just to stand and fight for this other thing that will save the whole 50,000?

Because ultimately when you’re put in these positions, you’re given these choices and the choice is: Fight for the thing that can actually do the most good. Build power toward that thing and then at least make your compromise better. The compromise should have been the public option, but that means you got to fight for something bigger.

You don’t have names, per se…

[Rep.] Ayanna Pressley. I think Ayanna Pressley and [Chris] Van Hollen. That’s where I would go right now.

Van Hollen, not exactly a bastion of progressivism…

No.
But you think he could do the job?

I think he could do the job.

And I think part of the thing here that’s just as important as getting a speaker that’s better or a leader that’s better is beating those people, flexing the power of the people. I’m not going to let the perfect be the enemy of the good here. I am going to let good be the enemy of bad. And I think that’s where we’re at right here.

My politics are definitely to the left of Van Hollen or whatever spectrum you want to look at. But I think the fight in and of itself is worth it, and winning is worth it.
