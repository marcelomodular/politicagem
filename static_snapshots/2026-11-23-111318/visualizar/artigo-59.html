Here’s this week’s question from a reader, condensed and edited for clarity:

My husband and I are not religious. He’s from an Episcopalian family and grew up in the Unitarian Universalist church, and I grew up attending an interdenominational church, but we don’t attend services now. Lately, though, he’s become so anxious about AI safety and the possibility of human extinction that he’s been having trouble sleeping. In the wake of all this existential uncertainty, he’s expressed a renewed interest in attending church. And he’s not interested in the UU church (with its believe-what-you-want attitude); he said he needs “the strong stuff.”

It’s all a bit mystifying to me because he does not believe in God. But I want him to feel better, and I don’t know what I or anyone can say about AI that will help him manage his anxiety. If religion is what he needs to find solace in the face of radical uncertainty, I want to support him. That said, I have misgivings about attending church myself and about us attending with our kids. I feel a bit self-conscious about being interlopers, and I also feel weird about him going alone every single weekend. More significantly, I wish I understood what the allure is for him. He says he’s hoping it will help him make peace with the end. I’m hoping there is something short of becoming churchgoers we can do that will help him find solace.

There are a couple of premises I would challenge your husband on, but the idea that religion can be supremely useful in a time like this is not one of them.

In fact, I think this is exactly what religion was made for — and not just because several religions actually grew out of apocalyptic movements (I’m looking at you, Christianity).

Faith has always focused on tackling the biggest moral and spiritual questions of the day. And in our day, lots of those questions center on technology. AI companies are striving to build a machine god, promising it’ll govern our world more intelligently, rationally, and morally than we ever could. Should we welcome that or cling to our own agency? Which kinds of labor should we outsource to AI, and which should we keep because they’re essential to human dignity? Where does human dignity come from, anyway?

These kinds of questions are the bread and butter of religion. And over the millennia, wisdom traditions of all kinds have had the chance to articulate answers, test and fine-tune them, and develop powerful stories around them that can move people to action. Whether you believe in God or not, I don’t think you need to feel weird about tapping into these wellsprings of wisdom. I think it’s a waste not to.

But there are two points on which I would gently challenge your husband. First, he said he doesn’t want to go to UU because he needs “the strong stuff.” I totally get this impulse — I also find UU services too “anything goes” for my taste — but don’t assume that the stricter a tradition is, the better an answer it has on AI.

Most people would consider Catholicism more hardcore than UU. And Pope Leo has strong opinions about AI, many of them great. Yet I actually think he’s fallen into a common “pro-human” trap: trying to argue that there are certain fixed traits that make humans unique, and locating value only in humans as they currently exist. He writes in his encyclical Magnifica Humanitas that “ours is the pressing duty to remain profoundly human.” But that feels insufficient as a guide to the future, because “human” has never been a static category — and it definitely won’t stay one. Instead, religious leaders should be offering us an answer to the question of what we want humanity to become in a tech-augmented world.

Second, your husband seems to be assuming that “the strong stuff” would be church services that help him “make peace with the end.” Services that look the grim reaper straight in the eye and accept that his scythe is coming for all of us, and fast. But it’s not a foregone conclusion that we’ll get an AI takeover resulting in human extinction!

Yes, AI companies are building this technology at a blistering pace. They are gambling with all of our futures as they race toward recursive self-improvement — AIs that can build the next, even smarter AIs themselves. Fear is an appropriate reaction to that.

This month, AI alignment pioneer Paul Christiano said he now believes there’s “a high risk of an AI takeover within the next three years,” meaning that AIs end up calling the shots on Earth. He believes that if we keep building the tech in the fastest, easiest way possible, “we’d be looking at something like a coin flip” — a 50 percent chance of takeover. Others have an even starker view: AI researcher and arch-doomer Eliezer Yudkowsky puts the odds of AI-driven human extinction at 99.5 percent.

But as plenty of experts have pointed out, it’s not clear that AIs will soon be able to outcompete and overpower humans across the board. For starters, AI research can suffer from diminishing returns: The easy discoveries get made first, and then everything you throw at it — more computing power, more experiments, more AI copies — moves things ahead less and less. What’s more, even though AI capabilities improve very fast on well-specified tasks in verifiable domains like math, the real world is messy. So it’s unclear how much their capabilities will generalize to other skills.

And when I investigated the case Yudkowsky makes, I found a lot of assumptions stacked on top of each other: that intelligence is a unitary property you can evenly maximize; that maximizing it in a machine means that machine has so much power it’s tantamount to magic; and that humans will just roll over and let AI grab all the power even as the risks become more glaring.

Should we acknowledge that there’s a chance of human extinction here and be appropriately scared of that? Yes. But when faced with a tower of assumptions, of “maybes” and “probablys” that compound, we should not treat extinction as a sure thing.

Instead, we should treat it as a possible thing, while treating other AI risks as extremely probable. I think it’s all too likely that AI could mess up our civilization not by staging an abrupt takeover but by gradually disempowering us and weakening the resilience of critical systems. Just imagine: Deepfakes and misinformation pollute our information ecosystem, so we’re barely capable of rational public discourse. Mass surveillance has a chilling effect on dissent, so democracy falters. Unemployment and wealth inequality skyrocket, so social unrest morphs into mobs and riots. Throw in some large-scale cyberattacks and blackouts, and you’ve got a recipe for global dysfunction.

If this is the default outcome we’re currently heading toward, then the implication isn’t that we should “make peace with the end.” It’s that we should fight tooth and nail to increase the resilience of each part of a functioning civilization!

And here’s where religion can really help. Religious traditions are packed with stories about people who fought to save the world, or who refused to give in to despair even when facing a high probability of disaster.

To illustrate, let me tell you a story from my own tradition, Judaism. It comes from the Talmud, and it’s about a child who out-argues the despair of all the adults.

When the Israelites are enslaved in Egypt, Pharaoh orders that every one of their baby boys be thrown into the Nile and killed. An Israelite leader named Amram leaves his wife, reasoning that there’s no point having children only to see them killed. Other men follow his lead. But then his young daughter, Miriam, confronts him: Dad, your verdict is even harsher than Pharaoh’s! His only targets boys; yours targets girls too. Plus, Pharaoh is wicked, so his decree may or may not come to pass, but you’re righteous, so yours certainly will.

Miriam’s probabilistic argument wins. Amram gets back together with his wife and, nine months later, Moses is born. Miriam doesn’t just passively wait for the Egyptians to kill the baby. She watches over him as he floats on the Nile in a little ark. When Pharaoh’s own daughter sees the baby and pities him, Miriam makes it possible for her to adopt him by casually mentioning that she knows a woman who can be his wet nurse (it happens to be her own mother). And so Moses survives, and grows up to lead his people out of slavery.

If I were a religious leader giving a sermon this weekend, this is the story I’d tell. It reminds us that even if you’re looking at a 50 percent chance of doom, the right move is not to just give up, which raises the odds to 100 percent. It’s to act bravely — and to use every shred of agency you’ve got to increase your community’s resilience and make your desired outcome more likely.

Of course, religion doesn’t have a monopoly on stories of moral courage. As the historian Ada Palmer recently pointed out, we’ve saved the world many times before: during the Cuban Missile Crisis, when we fixed the ozone layer, and so on. If it feels most natural to you to find inspiration in examples like these, go for it.

But religion does have a few comparative advantages. For one, its stories have incredible memetic power: They’ve spread across the globe and carry a huge amount of emotional and moral weight. That’s partly because these stories have been retold for centuries. But I think the causation also runs the other way. These stories have survived so long precisely because they encode something true and hard-won. And notice who their heroes tend to be. Not diplomats (like in the Cuban Missile Crisis) or atmospheric chemists (like in the ozone layer crisis), but a little girl arguing with her dad. Those are characters anyone can imagine becoming.

Crucially, religion also insists that something can always come along and deliver an outcome we didn’t predict. Typically, it calls that something “God.” But even if you don’t use that language, you can benefit from these nudges to remember that, however confident the forecast, the future is never fully knowable — and that’s a reason to act, not surrender.

So if your husband can find a congregation that reminds him of that, feeding his sense of agency instead of letting him acquiesce to despair, I say encourage him. And, unless you have an alternative wellspring that offers you the same benefits, maybe even consider joining him.
