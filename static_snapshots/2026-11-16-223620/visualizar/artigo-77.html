Título extraído: Being single can be a gift. Here’s how to embrace it.

When you’re single, it’s hard to avoid questions about your dating life. “Are you seeing anyone?” people will ask. “Had any fun dates?”

Well-meant as it may be, these questions can leave you feeling less than, like you’ve failed in something that you were supposed to have figured out by now. At their worst, frequent questions about your love life imply that you don’t have love at all.

Because we all learn, sooner or later, that there’s an expected direction of travel in life: Get an education; then a job. Fall in love and get married. Buy a house. Have children. And, of course, if you tick all those boxes, the expectation is that you’ll live happily ever after.

Our rational brains understand this is just a fairy tale. But the gravitational pull of its mythic expectations can feel impossible to withstand — hence those questions as you catch up with friends at happy hour or pass the cranberry sauce during the holidays. And it isn’t just social pressure; there are numerous policies and tax breaks that incentivize marriage and children. The architecture of our society is built for couples, so if you’re single — whether by choice or by circumstance — how should you live?

Who better to ask than a nun?

Anne Curtis is a Sister of Mercy and the executive director of Mercy by the Sea, a retreat center in Madison, Connecticut. As a young woman, she made a vow to dedicate her life to God, which meant remaining single; it didn’t mean withdrawing from love — but allowing for more of it.

If you’re imagining a stern old lady dressed in a habit, let me quickly disabuse you of that notion. For starters, Curtis, like most American nuns today, wears regular clothes. And she’s spent her life putting love for others into action. She campaigned for a living wage, affordable housing, and environmental protections through Network, a Catholic social justice lobbying group, and represented American nuns during a period of highly publicized tension with the more conservative Vatican.

In her, I see a model of singlehood that challenges the assumptions many of us have about being unpartnered, and the pervasive idea that coupledom equals love and singledom equals loneliness. First, Curtis has lived and worked among her community of sisters since entering the novitiate decades ago, building deep friendships. Second, without a partner, Curtis has been able to offer much more love to her family and friends, especially in times of trial.

“One of my sisters lost her spouse three years ago, and to this day, we check in every single day,” she says. “Just a really quick text. It’s part of, for me, entering into the day…. I sit in quiet, I pray, I walk the land here, I text my sister. I have a whole list of people who I specifically remember each day in my own prayer and reflection, because of something they’ve asked or some need they have.”

When I ask if she gives more love than she receives, Curtis challenges the assumptions of the question: “The more that I’ve understood and come in touch with the fact that I was created and came out of love, then I’m also able to offer that love [to others]. And it’s responded to.” The work of her faith is to ground herself in that reality. “The more genuine and authentic I am, the more love I experience,” she says.

This experience of belovedness enables Curtis to carry an awareness of, and tend to, the larger needs of the world. “I stand at the threshold of the land and the water and the sky,” she says. “I’m so aware of the expanse of the universe all around me and the worlds of Palestine, Ukraine, Sudan, Ethiopia. So many places where there’s incredible turmoil. And, I feel like, because of my life, I belong to a worldwide family.”

Of course, her life isn’t always easy. “When we say yes to something, we say no to a whole lot of other things,” she says. “It would be a lie to say I never felt the loneliness of my life choice or to say ‘I wonder what my life would have been like if…’” Particularly in her younger years, she found it difficult. But, with time, she realized this yearning was just part of the process — and it was wise to embrace what she had in all its joys and its struggles. “At some point in your life, it’s important to say yes to something,” she says.

James Martin understands this equally well. He’s a Jesuit priest and editor-at-large of America magazine. Similar to Curtis, he, too, took a vow of chastity and has therefore lived as a single person, a journey he documents in his memoir Work in Progress. Although he affirms that the Catholic Church actively promotes marriage, the “more basic is the idea that we’re meant for love.”

Think about that: Humans are designed for connection — and the way that love is given and received is different for each person, Martin says. Whether you find that in a partnership, in a community, in a family, or in service, what matters, according to this Catholic tradition, is that we love and are loved in return.

Should anyone doubt that, Martin laughs and says, “Let me just remind you…Jesus was single!”

“God calls us to be who we are,” Martin says. “You are called to be yourself in all of your beauty.” The question to ask, he says, is “How can I live out that calling in a loving way, and in a fruitful way and in a way that will help me to — one of my favorite words from Thomas Aquinas — help me to flourish?”

Martin does this in his own life via the Daily Examen. The Examen is a roughly 500-year-old prayer practice developed by the founder of the Jesuit order, St. Ignatius of Loyola. At the end of a day, maybe as you lay down to sleep, you go through your day hour by hour to notice the presence of God. If the word God gets in your way, try asking yourself, “Where did I notice love today?” It might be in the smile exchanged with a baby in the grocery aisle, while planning a friend’s birthday dinner, or getting a hug from your mom. If you’ve never tried it before, give it a go. It’s a powerful practice of gratitude and trains you to notice the beauty in your life. In my experience, the Examen helps me practice what Martin describes as having what you have, rather than focusing on what you don’t.

Naturally, that shift can take time. Brianna Cayo Cotter, a social impact executive in her 40s who is single, has noticed this change over decades, too.

“In my 20s, I was dating lots of different people,” she says, “figuring out…heartbreak and highs and lows.” Then, in her 30s, she felt a longing and a yearning as she saw a lot of people coupling off. “And that felt…like a loss at the time.” But now she’s come to a great acceptance — partly because she’s seen that marriage is no guarantee to stave off loneliness, and comes with many of its own burdens. “I think my life is actually better and more rich and has more love and companionship in it than most of the couples that I know,” she says. “Not all! But, really most.” So many coupled friends struggle to build community in their forties, for example. “I can empathize, but I cannot relate at all.”

But more than this, being single gives Cayo Cotter more time. “More time in your life to be juicy and spread more love and deepen it and have profoundly intimate relationships with lots of different people in different ways.” Whether she’s traveling with others, hosting friends, or getting together with neighbors on her block, Cayo Cotter lives a life in which she is wildly loved.

Of course, she still gets those questions. “I remember my mom, when she was visiting me one time, she was like, ‘But who would go and pick you up when you get your colonoscopy?’” Cayo Cotter says with a laugh. “I was like, ‘I could ask 20 people to do that!’” Because she gives love so generously, she receives it bountifully, too.

And despite not being a nun, or even religiously affiliated, this practice of loving and being loved has a spiritual quality for Cayo Cotter. She practices yoga daily, and says one closing prayer really resonates with her: “The light in me sees the light in you. And the more that you can go out into the world with a greater light shining from you… that is how to live.”

Curtis would agree: “Whatever your faith dimension is or practice or belief, I think they all, in some way, are a call to love. … There’s limitless ways to offer love and be loved.”

So when people ask whether you’re dating anyone, you might want to answer the bigger question that they’re prodding for: “No, I’m not partnered. But I am very loved.”
