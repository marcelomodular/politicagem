Título extraído: Chris Van Hollen talks about challenging Chuck Schumer for Senate majority leader, Israel, Iran, and more.

Change is coming to Washington, DC, next Congress — and one Democratic senator thinks that that should also mean a change in leadership for the new Senate majority.

Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-MD) is challenging Sen. Chuck Schumer (D-NY), who has led the party’s caucus in the upper chamber for a decade, for the top job in the new Congress. Van Hollen, who previously served in the House before being elected to the Senate in 2016, said the party needs a fresh face to deliver the change voters demanded when they handed Dems such big victories in the midterms.

In an interview on Vox’s America, Actually podcast, Van Hollen told host Astead Herndon that Schumer has been too timid in confronting Trump and the Republican Party, and too unwilling to use unconventional means to try to rally support for Democratic causes.

“My view is that working outside the Congress to highlight the attacks on our democracy was essential. You can’t just use the same tools that were used before Trump to take on a lawless Trump,” Van Hollen said. “We need to organize in this moment to help protect our democracy and our Constitution and get things done and deliver.”

Herndon spoke with Van Hollen ahead of the December 1 vote that will decide the chamber’s Democratic leadership for the new Congress; below is an excerpt of their, edited for length and clarity.

There’s much more in the full show, including how Van Hollen would handle the looming government funding deadline and a possible shutdown over the Iran war; his approach to reining in funding for Israel and finding a path forward to improve the US healthcare system; and his plans for holding the Trump administration accountable.

You’re running for Senate majority leader, challenging Chuck Schumer. What pushed you to raise your hand?

When I saw the results of this election, which was a sweeping crying out from the American people about the need for change, my view was that this was clearly a rejection of the policies of Donald Trump. But Democrats were still on probation, and I don’t think we earned people’s trust by simply going back to the status quo.

We’ve been given this big opportunity, with 56 senators. We cannot blow it, and we need to show people that we’re going to deliver and fight for them.

You’ve also been in office a long time. You’ve been close to the Democratic establishment. How do the politics of Chris Von Hollen represent what the Democratic base was crying out for more than the politics of Chuck Schumer?

If you look at my public service, there are many times that I have taken on the Democratic establishment, but you’re right. I was part of Speaker Pelosi’s leadership team in the House.

This election, in my view, was a message of economic populism: People saying they want to take on a system that’s rigged in favor of the very wealthy, against working people, and fueled by political corruption. My record has been taking on big money special interests for the public good.

If you look at all the issues our candidates ran on, there’s going to be an army of lobbyists descending on Washington, DC, in the Capitol to eat away at what Democrats said they wanted to do. My view is you need someone with a record of taking on those interests at this very important moment.

“This should not be a one-man band. We need more of our team having meaningful roles to deliver for the American people.”

Are the issues with Schumer one of tactics or one of communication? Where have his failings been?

If you look at the issues that we want to advance right now, which is taking on a tax code that’s rigged in favor of people who make money off of money and against people who earn a paycheck through hard work, or the ambitious changes that we need to make in our very broken healthcare system — which has become a money-making system, not a healthcare system — then it’s important that you have somebody leading the Democratic caucus who has been fighting for those issues every single day.

I will also say I don’t think we want a Democratic leader who intervenes in open, contested Democratic primaries. I’ve got two colleagues, senators-elect from Michigan and from Minnesota, who would not be there if Senator Schumer had had his way because he sided with AIPAC and crypto and other special interests like Big Pharma, in the case of Michigan, against a candidate who was more the people’s candidate.

I don’t think we should be interfering in those primaries, and I also think we need more democracy in the United States Senate, and I think my colleagues agree.

Can you give me an example of a negotiation that Schumer has handled in Trump’s second term that you would do differently?

I do believe that early on in this administration many of us recognized the threat that this administration posed to our democracy and to our Constitution. That was around the time that the Trump administration abducted a constituent of mine, Kilmar Abrego Garcia, and took him down to a gulag in El Salvador. I went down there to see if he was dead or alive and ultimately bring him back, in order to protect his due process rights.

My view is that there should have been much more intense resistance from the Senate early on in those days. That would have meant early on confronting the president, and refusing to provide additional money and funding for some of his lawless activities.

What does that look like?

That would be essentially saying, “Mr. President, we’re not going to give you this blank check in order to carry out these attacks on our Constitution, to see these ICE raids where they were undermining constitutional rights, killing American citizens in the streets of Minneapolis.”

All of that needed to be resisted early on. I helped organize the first protest demonstration against Elon Musk taking his chainsaw to USAID. We also went to highlight that lawlessness in front of other federal agencies. My view is that working outside the Congress to highlight the attacks on our democracy was essential. You can’t just use the same tools that were used before Trump to take on a lawless Trump.

It sounds like you’re talking about making the position more of an activist organizer position and leading from the front more than we saw Schumer do over the last couple years.

That is fair. I do think that, especially with our new members. They need to be front and center. But we need to put everyone’s talents forward.

The bottom line is, we need to organize in this moment to help protect our democracy and our Constitution and get things done and deliver.

The magic number for any Senate leader is 28. Chuck Schumer has said he has 16 declared supporters. I think that includes himself. You have five. What is the whip count?

We’re now north of 15. We have momentum in our favor. Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan supported me, which shows that this is not a progressive versus moderate thing. It really is a question of taking on an economy rigged for the wealthy against working people. That is the framework in which our candidates pursued this election.

I think that that is what unifies us all going forward. But to do that, it is important to have someone with a history and a record of taking on big money special interests to advance the public interest.

North of 15 is still 13 short of 28, though, if my math is correct.

You’re right. There are a lot of people who are very undecided. The longer this debate goes on, the discussion goes on, the more people want to change how we organize ourselves in the caucus. This should not be a one-man band. We need more of our team having meaningful roles to deliver for the American people.
